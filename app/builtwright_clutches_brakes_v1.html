Clutches and Brakes
Module 19
A clutch connects, a brake stops, and both turn energy into heat through friction
A clutch transmits torque between a driver and a driven shaft on command; a brake absorbs the kinetic energy of a moving load and holds it stopped. Both use friction surfaces pressed together by a spring, air, oil, or a magnet, and both wear those surfaces at a rate set by the energy per engagement and the number of engagements. The three questions on any clutch or brake are: is the air gap or adjustment correct, is the friction material within wear limits, and is the actuator (spring, coil, cylinder) delivering the force it should. A brake that holds a suspended load is also a safety device and is treated as one.
Where they are and what they do
  • Motor brakes: a spring-applied, electrically released disc brake on the motor NDE. Power off, the brake sets; power on, the coil pulls the armature and releases it. Hoists, cranes, conveyors on inclines, indexers, anything that must stop and stay stopped.
  • Clutch-brake units: on presses, packaging machines, and indexers, a combined unit that connects the flywheel to the crank and stops the crank on demand, air or electrically actuated, cycling many times a minute.
  • Overload clutches: torque limiters that slip or disengage when the load exceeds a setting, protecting the drive from a jam. Ball detent, friction, and shear pin types.
  • Backstops: one-way clutches on inclined conveyors and elevators that let the drive turn forward and lock against reverse rotation when the drive stops.
  • Fluid couplings and soft-start clutches: let the motor start unloaded and pick the load up smoothly; the Couplings module covers the fluid coupling.
  • Centrifugal clutches: engage as speed rises; small engines and some fans.
Field tip: a brake that smells is a brake that is draggingThe smell of hot friction material from a motor brake with the motor running is a brake that has not fully released: the air gap is closed up by wear, the coil is weak or on the wrong voltage, or the rectifier has failed. It is eating itself and heating the motor, and when the lining is gone the load drops. Infrared on the brake housing on the PM; hotter than the motor is dragging.

Friction material: the consumable that decides everything
  • Materials: organic (resin bonded fibre, the common one), sintered metallic (high energy, high temperature), and specialty (paper for wet clutches, ceramic). Asbestos was the fibre until the 1990s; old linings and old spare linings in stores are treated as asbestos until proven otherwise (Seals module Safety tab has the handling rule).
  • Energy per engagement: the kinetic energy of the load stopped, or the slip energy during clutch engagement, all becomes heat in the friction surfaces. Too much energy per stop, or too many stops per hour, overheats the material: it glazes, fades (loses friction when hot), cracks, or chars. The manufacturer rates the unit for energy per cycle and cycles per hour.
  • Wear limit: a minimum lining thickness, or a maximum air gap, on the nameplate or in the manual. Measured on the PM and replaced before the limit. A lining worn to the rivets or the backing plate scores the mating surface and the brake fails to hold.
  • Glazing: a shiny hard surface from overheating or from light dragging. Reduced friction, the brake slips or the clutch will not transmit. Light sanding restores it once; repeated glazing means the cause (dragging, overload) is still there.
  • Contamination: oil or grease on a dry friction surface cuts the friction to a fraction. A leaking shaft seal on the motor or gearbox next to a brake is a brake that will not hold. Wet clutches are the opposite: they need the specified oil, and the wrong oil (wrong friction modifier) makes them chatter or slip.
  • Mating surface: the disc, drum, or armature plate wears too. Scoring, heat checking (a network of fine cracks), bluing, and out-of-flat all reduce contact. Resurface or replace to the manufacturer limit.
  • Bedding in: new linings need a run-in at reduced load to seat to the mating surface; a new brake at full duty on day one glazes.
Adjustment and air gap
Every friction clutch and brake has a gap between the friction surfaces when disengaged, and that gap grows as the lining wears. Too large and a spring-applied brake has less spring force (the springs are less compressed) and a magnetic brake may not pull the armature across at all: the brake fails to release or, worse, fails to set fully. Too small and it drags. The manufacturer specifies a nominal gap and a maximum; adjustment brings it back to nominal, and when the adjustment range is used up the lining is replaced.
  • Electromagnetic disc brakes: air gap measured with a feeler gauge between the armature plate and the magnet body, at three or four points around the circumference, with the brake set (power off). Typical nominal 0.2 to 0.5 mm; maximum around 0.6 to 1.0 mm depending on size. Adjust with the sleeve nuts or the adjusting screws per the manual; equal gap all round.
  • Manual release levers on motor brakes: for moving a load by hand under controlled conditions; they must be returned to the released-off position or the brake is disabled.
  • Drum brakes: shoe clearance to the drum, equal on both shoes, set by the adjusting rods. Thruster or solenoid stroke checked so the brake fully releases at the end of the stroke and fully sets when de-energised.
  • Air clutch-brake units: air pressure at the unit against the specification (a regulator that has been turned down is a clutch that slips), clearance per the manual, and the quick-exhaust valves that give a fast set on the brake side.
  • Torque limiters: the slip torque setting, by the spring compression dimension or the manufacturer's torque chart, verified with a torque wrench or a known load. A limiter set high to stop nuisance trips is a drive with no protection.
  • After adjustment: a functional test. Release and set with the load blocked, then a controlled test with the load, then the gap rechecked after the first shift.
  • Record the gap and the lining thickness every PM; the trend is the wear rate and the replacement date.
Field tip: three feeler readings, not oneAn armature plate that measures 0.3 mm at one spot and 0.8 mm opposite is cocked, and it will drag on the tight side and be weak on the loose side. Measure at three or four points, adjust for equal gap, then set the nominal. A brake with a rectifier or a coil fault can show the same symptom as a bad gap; check the voltage at the coil before condemning the mechanics.
Holding brakes and backstops: when the brake is the only thing between the load and the floor
A holding brake on a hoist, a lift, or an inclined conveyor is a safety device
It holds a suspended load with the power off, every night, with nobody watching. It is inspected, tested, and adjusted on a schedule as a safety device, and any work on it is done with the load lowered or mechanically supported. A holding brake is never adjusted, released, or opened with a load hanging on it. Cranes and hoists in Ontario fall under Regulation 851 section 51 and CSA B167; the brake is part of the inspection.
  • Holding test: with the load at rest and the brake set, the drive is commanded (or the load is applied) and the brake must hold without creep. Some hoists have a brake test mode; others are tested with a rated test load. The manual sets the method and the interval.
  • Dual brakes: many hoists have a motor brake and a second load brake (a mechanical load brake in the gear train, or a second disc brake). Each is tested on its own; a hoist that holds on two brakes is not proving either one.
  • Backstops: sprag or roller ramp one-way clutches on the reducer or the head shaft of an inclined conveyor. They allow forward rotation and lock against reverse. Inspected for oil condition (some run in the gearbox oil, some are grease packed), for the correct direction of installation (a backstop fitted backward locks the drive), and tested by stopping the loaded conveyor and watching for runback.
  • A backstop is not a lockout. It holds the load against runback while the drive train is intact. Open the coupling or the gearbox and the load is free. Block the load before drive work.
  • Brake release for maintenance: the manual release lever or a screw on the brake lets the load be lowered under control. It is used with the load supported or the descent controlled, and it is returned to normal before the machine is released.
  • Coil voltage and rectifier: a DC brake coil fed through a rectifier from AC. A failed rectifier or low voltage is a brake that releases slowly or not at all, drags, and overheats.
Clutch and brake fault diagnosis

Load supported, locked out. Then: air gap or clearance at three points against the specification. Lining thickness against the minimum. Mating surface condition. Contamination (oil, water). Manual release lever position. Then the actuator: coil voltage and rectifier, air pressure at the unit, spring condition, thruster stroke.

Running (from a safe position): smell and infrared for dragging; noise on engagement (chatter, squeal, bang); time to stop or to engage; creep with the brake set.

The three questions resolve almost all of it. A brake or clutch with correct gap, good lining, and a working actuator that still misbehaves is being asked for more energy than it is rated for.

Safety: the load, the springs, and the heat
Releasing a brake releases whatever it was holding
A brake is opened, adjusted, or released only when the load it holds is lowered to rest or supported by something that does not depend on the brake. A spring-applied brake has compressed springs inside it that unload when the cover comes off. A clutch that engages unexpectedly connects a running motor to whatever your hands are near. Lockout, load control, and spring energy every time.
  • Suspended loads: lowered, cribbed, or pinned before brake work. Hoists and cranes: the manufacturer's procedure and Regulation 851 section 51.
  • Spring energy: brake springs are compressed in the assembly. Disassembly per the manual, with the compression released in a controlled way (a press, or the adjusting screws backed off in sequence). Springs that let go are projectiles.
  • Electrical: the brake coil and rectifier are on the motor supply or a separate circuit. Lockout covers both. A brake coil is a DC circuit that can hold a charge in a large coil for a moment after switch-off.
  • Air clutch-brake units: the air is stored energy. Isolate and vent; the quick exhaust valves do not vent the supply side.
  • Heat: a brake after a heavy stop or a dragging brake is hot enough to burn. Infrared before hands.
  • Friction dust: old linings may be asbestos. Wet cleaning, no compressed air, and the asbestos procedure for anything unknown. Modern linings still produce dust that should not be breathed.
  • Manual release levers returned to the off position before the machine is handed back. A brake left released is a load that drops on the first stop.
  • Test with the load: after any brake work, a holding test and a controlled stop test before the machine returns to service, with nobody under the load.
