Machine Safeguarding and PSR
Module 14
Safeguarding is a machine system, and millwrights build it, modify it, and defeat it
Every guard in the plant was made by someone in the trade. Every interlock was wired to a machine a millwright installed. Every guard that is missing was taken off by someone who needed to get at the machine and did not put it back. This module covers what the law requires in Ontario, what the standards say a guard has to be, how to size an opening or a distance, how a risk assessment decides what level of safeguarding is needed, and what a pre-start health and safety review is and when one is required. It is written for the person who has to make the guard, not for the engineer who signs the report, and the two need to understand each other.
The hierarchy of controls, applied to machines
LevelWhat it means for a machineExample
1. EliminateDesign the hazard outReplace a nip point with a drive that has no exposed rotating parts; move the operator task away from the hazard
2. SubstituteA less hazardous version of the same thingLower pressure, lower speed, smaller stored energy, a safer process
3. Engineering controls: guards and devicesPhysical barriers and safeguarding devices that do not depend on the personFixed guards, interlocked guards, light curtains, two-hand controls
4. AwarenessWarnings, signs, markings, awareness barriersSignage on a guard, floor marking, an awareness rope
5. Administrative controls and PPEProcedures, training, lockout, personal protective equipmentLockout procedure, safe work practice, gloves and glasses
  • The higher on the list, the better the control. A sign is not a guard. A procedure is not a guard. Lockout protects maintenance; it does not protect the operator during production.
  • Guards and devices are the millwright's territory. The design decision (which level, which type) comes from the risk assessment, and the construction comes from the standard.
  • Ontario Regulation 851 sections 24, 25, 26, 28, 31, and 32 set the legal minimum for machine guarding in industrial establishments. CSA Z432 is the standard that says how to meet it. ISO 12100, 13849, 13857, and 14120 are the international standards Z432 draws from.
Field tip: the guard that gets left off was designed wrongA guard that takes twenty bolts and two people to remove, and has to come off for a five-minute belt check, will be found leaning against the wall. That is a design failure, not a discipline failure. An inspection window, a hinged and interlocked access door, or a tension check port makes the guard something people leave on. Build guards for the maintenance that will happen, not the maintenance that should.

Hazard identification: how to walk a machine
  • Walk it in every mode: normal running, setup, changeover, cleaning, jam clearing, maintenance. The hazards that hurt people are the ones present during the tasks nobody planned for.
  • Follow the energy: every motor, every cylinder, every spring, every raised load, every pressurised line. Where does each one move something, and where could a person be when it does.
  • Look at the pinch points at the machine's own scale: a 5 mm gap that closes is a fingertip; a 100 mm gap is a hand; a 500 mm gap is a body.
  • Ask who reaches in, where, and why. The reasons are the tasks that need a safe method, not a sign.
  • Photograph and list every hazard with its location, the task that exposes someone to it, and the current control. That list is the input to the risk assessment and the PSR.

Guard construction: what the standard asks for
  • Strong enough to withstand the forces it may see: a person leaning or falling on it, ejected parts, the machine itself. Expanded metal, welded wire mesh, sheet, polycarbonate, or perforated plate on a frame. Plastic sheet that flexes into a hazard is not a guard.
  • No new hazards: rounded edges, no sharp corners, no pinch at the hinge, no projections, no trap between guard and machine.
  • Openings sized to the reach distance to the nearest hazard (Distances and Openings tab). Small openings near the hazard; larger openings only far from it.
  • Fixed guards are fastened with fasteners that need a tool and that stay captive to the guard or the machine when it is opened, so they are not lost and the guard goes back on.
  • Full enclosure of the hazard, including the return side, the underside, and the ends. A belt guard that covers the front and leaves the back open is half a guard.
  • Visibility: mesh or clear panels where the operator needs to see the process. A guard that has to be removed to see the process will be.
  • Maintenance access designed in: inspection windows, hinged doors (interlocked if they open without a tool), tension check ports, grease fitting extensions to the outside.
  • Labelled and painted: a consistent guard colour makes a missing guard obvious from across the plant.
Field tip: bring the grease fitting outsideThe most common reason a guard is off during operation is a grease fitting behind it. A length of hose and a remote fitting block on the outside of the guard removes the reason. Same for oil sight glasses: a window in the guard in line with the glass. Design the guard so the PM can be done through it.

Performance level and categories: the short version
ISO 13849-1 rates a safety function by how reliably it performs. Performance Level (PL) a through e is the required reliability from the risk assessment; Category B, 1, 2, 3, 4 is the circuit architecture that achieves it. A single switch and a contactor is Category 1 and gets you PL c at best. Two channels, monitored, with a safety relay is Category 3 or 4 and reaches PL d or e. The risk assessment decides the required PL. The design and the components decide what is delivered.
  • Category 3: a single fault does not cause loss of the safety function, and most faults are detected. Two channels (two contacts on the interlock, two contactors), monitored by a safety relay. The common target for guarding on production machines.
  • Category 4: as 3, plus a single fault is detected at or before the next demand and an accumulation of faults does not cause loss of the function. The target where the harm is severe and frequent.
  • An interlock switch, a safety relay, and two contactors with mirror contacts fed back to the relay is the standard Category 3 guard circuit. A millwright wires the switch and the contactors; the electrician and the safety system designer own the circuit.
  • Never add an interlock to a machine by wiring it into the PLC stop input. A standard PLC is not a safety device. The interlock goes through a safety relay or safety PLC that removes power from the actuators.
Safe distances and guard openings (from ISO 13857 and Z432)

Safety distance for a light curtain or other presence-sensing device
S = K × T + C
S = minimum distance from the detection plane to the hazard
K = approach speed. ISO 13855 rule for a vertical curtain: calculate first with K = 2000 mm/s; if the result is over 500 mm, recalculate with K = 1600 mm/s and use that (minimum 500 mm). If the first result is 500 mm or less, use it (minimum 100 mm)
T = total stopping time: device response + safety logic response + machine stopping time (measured with a stop-time meter)
C = penetration factor: for curtains with resolution d up to 40 mm, C = 8 × (d minus 14) mm, minimum 0

Example: 30 mm resolution curtain, total stop time 0.35 s
C = 8 × (30 minus 14) = 128 mm
First pass: S = 2000 × 0.35 + 128 = 828 mm, which is over 500
Second pass: S = 1600 × 0.35 + 128 = 688 mm. Mount the curtain at least 688 mm from the hazard
  • T includes the machine's actual stopping time, measured, not the drive's nominal ramp. A press brake or a conveyor with a heavy load coasts. A stop-time measurement is part of every light curtain installation and every PSR that involves one.
  • A curtain mounted closer than S does not protect anyone; a hand reaches the hazard before the machine stops. The calculation is the installation.
  • Resolution 14 mm detects a finger; 30 mm a hand; 70 mm an arm. Coarser resolution means a larger C and a greater distance.
  • Reaching over, under, or around the curtain must be prevented by the mounting height and width or by additional guarding. The curtain only protects the plane it covers.
Two-hand control distance
Same formula with C = 250 mm and K = 1600 mm/s: S = 1600 × T + 250. Buttons far enough apart and shrouded so they cannot be operated with one hand and an elbow, and both must be released and re-pressed for each cycle (anti-tie-down).
Risk assessment: how the level of safeguarding is decided
ISO 12100 and CSA Z1002 describe the process: identify the hazards, estimate the risk from each (how badly it could hurt someone, how often someone is exposed, how likely the harmful event is, and whether they could avoid it), evaluate whether that risk is acceptable, and if not, reduce it using the hierarchy of controls, then check again. The output is a documented list of hazards with the risk before and after controls, and the safeguarding that was chosen for each. The ISO 13849 risk graph turns the same inputs into a required Performance Level for any safety function.
ParameterOptionsWhat to think about
Severity (S)S1 slight, reversible; S2 serious, irreversible or fatalAmputation, crushing, and entanglement are S2. A bruise is S1.
Frequency and duration of exposure (F)F1 seldom or short; F2 frequent or continuousOnce a shift for a moment versus every cycle. Include maintenance and clearing.
Possibility of avoidance (P)P1 possible under some conditions; P2 scarcely possibleSpeed of the hazard, whether it can be seen coming, whether the person can get clear.
Result: required PLS1 F1 P1 gives PL a; S2 F2 P2 gives PL eThe risk graph in ISO 13849-1 Annex A maps the three choices to a required PL from a to e.
  • Assess by task, not just by machine. The same nip point is a different risk during production (guarded) and during a jam clearance (guard open, machine live).
  • Do it with the people who run and maintain the machine in the room. They know the tasks and the workarounds.
  • Residual risk after controls is documented and communicated: signage, training, and procedures cover what the guards cannot.
  • The assessment is the evidence for the PSR. An engineer reviewing a machine wants to see the hazard list, the ratings, and the controls, with the reasoning.
Field tip: the jam clearance task decides the guardAsk how the machine gets unjammed. If the answer involves a hand in a gap with the machine energised, that task sets the safeguarding requirement for the whole machine, no matter how well production mode is guarded. The right answer is an interlocked door with guard locking and a safe clearing method, and it is cheaper than the alternative.
Pre-Start Health and Safety Review: Ontario Regulation 851, section 7
What a PSR is
A pre-start health and safety review is a review, required by section 7 of Regulation 851 (Industrial Establishments), of a new or modified apparatus, structure, protective element, or process before it is started, to confirm it meets the applicable requirements of the regulation. It is done by a professional engineer, results in a signed report identifying the measures needed for compliance, and the employer must complete those measures before the equipment starts. The section applies to specific circumstances listed in a table in the regulation, and it has specific exemptions. Read the current section; this tab is a field summary, not the law.
When section 7 is triggered: the eight circumstances in the table
The table in section 7 lists the situations. In plain language, a PSR is required before starting or resuming operation when the new or modified installation involves:
  • Flammable liquids being located or dispensed in a building, room, or area (relates to section 22).
  • A process that involves a risk of ignition or explosion and creates a condition of imminent hazard (relates to sections 63 and 65).
  • A rack or stacking structure for storage (relates to section 45, and CSA A344 is the referenced standard for steel storage racks).
  • A process that uses or produces a substance that could expose a worker above an occupational exposure limit, requiring ventilation or other controls (relates to sections 127 and 128).
  • A factory that produces aluminum or steel, or a foundry, with respect to specific melting and pouring hazards (relates to sections 68 and 70).
  • A process involving a dust that could cause a dust explosion, with respect to the dust collection or control (relates to section 65).
  • A machine with a protective element or safeguarding device, where the guarding is what section 7 is reviewing (relates to sections 24, 25, 26, 28, 31, and 32). This is the circumstance that covers most machine guarding PSRs and most of the work a millwright will be involved in.
  • A lifting device, travelling crane, or automobile hoist (relates to sections 51 and 53).
Two of these, racking and machine guarding, are the everyday ones in a manufacturing plant, and they are where a millwright's field knowledge is the substance of the report.
Who, what, and the exemptions
  • Who: a professional engineer conducts the review and signs and seals the report. The engineer may rely on measurements, inspection, and documentation provided by others, which is where the millwright comes in.
  • What the report contains: the details of the circumstances, the measures necessary to comply with the applicable sections, and a statement of whether they have been completed. The employer keeps it available, and the joint health and safety committee or representative gets a copy.
  • Exemptions: section 7 sets out limited circumstances where a PSR is not required. In general terms they cover an apparatus that was already reviewed and has not been modified in a way that affects the reviewed elements, and certain cases where the equipment is certified as complying with a listed standard and installed per the manufacturer. The exemptions are narrow and specific; the regulation and the Ministry of Labour guideline are the authority on whether one applies.
  • Modification counts: moving a machine, changing its guarding, changing its controls, adding a device, or changing the process it runs can all trigger a new review. A plant that moved a press and reused the old PSR has not complied.
  • The PSR is a review of compliance with Regulation 851. It is not a substitute for a risk assessment, a commissioning check, or a functional test of the safety system, though a good PSR will look at all three.
The millwright's role in a PSR
  • Before the engineer arrives: the machine drawings, the manufacturer's manual and declaration, the hazard walk (Hazards tab), the risk assessment if one exists, the stop-time measurement on any presence-sensing device, and photographs of every guard and device.
  • During the review: walk the machine with the engineer in every mode. Show the tasks: setup, changeover, clearing, maintenance. Point out the guard that comes off and why.
  • Measurements: guard openings and distances (Distances tab), fence heights, curtain mounting distances, interlock function tests, e-stop coverage, guard fastening.
  • After the report: the measures listed are the work order. Build the guards to Z432, wire the devices through safety-rated logic, document the completion, and get the engineer's confirmation before the machine starts.
  • Racking: for a rack PSR under CSA A344, the millwright inspects installation against the drawings, anchoring, plumb, damage, load signage, and configuration versus the design. The Rack Grid and PSR Inspector tools cover this in the field.
Field tip: the PSR is easier when the millwright did the homeworkAn engineer who arrives to a machine with a hazard list, photos of every guard, measured openings, a stop-time number, and a drawing of the safety circuit writes the report in an afternoon. The same engineer arriving to "it's over there" spends the first day doing the millwright's job at an engineer's rate. The homework is also the record that proves the plant knew what it was doing.
Safeguarding problems in the field

A guard that is off, an interlock that is jumpered, a light curtain that is muted with tape: none of these are the problem. They are what someone did to solve a problem the safeguarding created for them. Find that problem and the safeguard stays on.

Ask what task needed it defeated. Greasing, clearing, watching the process, threading a web, adjusting a guide. Then design for the task: a port, a window, a hold-to-run mode, an interlocked door with locking.

Nuisance trips are the other reason safeguards get defeated. A curtain that stops the line ten times a shift on dust, a scanner that sees a forklift, an interlock that vibrates open. Fix the nuisance and the tape comes off.

Never restore a defeated safeguard without finding out why it was defeated. It will be defeated again tomorrow, by the same person, for the same reason.

Working on safeguarding systems
The person testing the safeguard is standing where it is supposed to protect someone
Functional testing of an interlock, a light curtain, or a two-hand control means operating the machine with the safeguard in the loop and checking that it stops. The tester is at the hazard. Test with a test piece (a rod for the curtain, a hand-shaped block for the two-hand shroud), never with a body part, and with the machine in a mode where the worst-case failure of the safeguard cannot reach anyone.
Lockout versus safeguarding
  • An interlocked guard is a production safeguard. It is not a lockout. Opening an interlocked door and reaching in is protected only by the safety circuit, which can fail, and only if guard locking holds the door until the hazard has stopped.
  • Maintenance inside a machine is done under lockout, Regulation 851 section 75 and 76, CSA Z460. The interlock is a backup, not the isolation.
  • Building or modifying a guard on a machine: the machine is locked out. A guard that is being fitted is a guard that is not yet protecting anyone.
  • Testing after modification: a written test procedure, a second person, and a mode where a safeguard failure does not injure the tester.
Safety circuits are not to be improvised
  • Jumpering an interlock, muting a curtain, or bridging a safety relay channel "just to test" removes the protection for everyone, not just the person who did it. If a safeguard must be bypassed for a task, that task needs a designed mode (hold-to-run, reduced speed, enabling device) built into the safety system by whoever is qualified to modify it.
  • Wiring changes to a safety circuit are done by someone qualified on the system, documented, and followed by a functional test of every safety function on the machine, not just the one that changed.
  • A safety relay or safety PLC fault that is reset repeatedly without diagnosis is a fault that will eventually be a failure to stop.
Fabricating guards
  • Cutting and welding guard material: standard hot work and metalworking hazards. Expanded metal edges are sharp; grind and deburr before handling.
  • Polycarbonate panels: the right material for impact and visibility, and it must be the right grade and thickness for the ejection hazard. Acrylic shatters.
  • A guard is a structure. Hinges, latches, and frames sized so the guard does not sag, fall, or trap fingers.
  • Paint colour: consistent for guards across the plant, so a missing one is visible.
