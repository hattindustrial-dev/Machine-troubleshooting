What misalignment actually does
Misalignment is cited as a root cause in the majority of premature bearing, seal, and coupling failures in rotating machinery. Understanding why requires thinking about what happens inside the machine rather than just what is visible at the coupling. A misaligned machine is not simply running slightly imperfectly. It is applying forces that were never part of the design intent, on every single revolution, for every hour it operates.
What it does to bearings
Bearings are designed to carry specific load directions. A radial bearing carries radial load. A thrust bearing carries axial load. Misalignment imposes load in directions the bearing was not designed to handle, or concentrates load on one side of the race rather than distributing it evenly. The result is accelerated fatigue of the race surface, elevated operating temperature, and a service life that can be a fraction of the rated life. When the bearing fails, it looks like a lubrication failure or an overload failure, because the misalignment itself is rarely visible by the time the bearing is on the bench.
What it does to seals
Mechanical seals and shaft seals are designed to operate with the shaft running true. Misalignment causes the shaft to describe an arc rather than rotate on a fixed axis, which means the seal faces are constantly being deflected and released on every revolution. This generates heat at the seal faces, causes uneven wear, and eventually destroys the sealing surface. A mechanical seal that leaks shortly after installation, with no obvious installation error, is often the result of misalignment that was present before the seal was installed.
What it does to the coupling
A flexible coupling can accommodate a small amount of misalignment. This is not the same as saying misalignment is acceptable. The coupling's flexible element (spider, disc pack, grid) is compensating for the misalignment on every revolution. It is being cyclically loaded and unloaded, compressed and released. The heat and fatigue generated by this cycle degrades the flexible element far faster than it would degrade in a correctly aligned machine. Black rubber dust inside the coupling guard is not just a coupling problem. It is a misalignment indicator.
What it does to the whole machine
Misalignment generates a vibration signature that travels through the machine frame and into connected equipment. In a pump system, vibration from a misaligned coupling can cause cavitation-like symptoms, accelerate wear on impeller wear rings, and transmit into piping, loosening flanged connections over time. It also increases the load on the motor, raising current draw and operating temperature. A machine that consistently trips its motor overload, runs hot, or chews through couplings and bearings is very often a misalignment problem wearing a different mask.
  • Industry data consistently shows that 50 percent or more of rotating equipment failures have misalignment as a contributing cause
  • The cost of a laser alignment job is a fraction of the cost of a bearing replacement, a seal replacement, and an unplanned shutdown
  • Alignment should be verified after any bearing replacement, any coupling replacement, any base repair, and any time the machine is moved or reconnected to piping
  • Thermal growth: a machine aligned cold may be misaligned at operating temperature if thermal growth was not accounted for. Critical machines should be aligned at operating temperature or with calculated thermal offsets.

Parallel (offset) misalignment: centrelines parallel but not coincident
Parallel misalignment (also called offset misalignment) means the two shaft centrelines are parallel to each other but not on the same axis. Looking at the coupling end-on, one shaft is displaced vertically, horizontally, or both relative to the other. This is measured as the perpendicular distance between the two centrelines and is corrected by moving one machine up, down, left, or right (shimming and lateral adjustment).
  • Generates a vibration at 2x rotational frequency, which is a useful diagnostic indicator
  • Causes the coupling flexible element to deflect twice per revolution (once at top, once at bottom of the offset cycle)
  • Vertical parallel misalignment is corrected with shims under the machine feet
  • Horizontal parallel misalignment is corrected by lateral movement of the machine on its base
  • Acceptable tolerance depends on coupling type and shaft speed. Always refer to the coupling manufacturer's specification.
Angular misalignment: centrelines meet at an angle across the coupling
Angular misalignment means the two shaft centrelines are not parallel. They converge or diverge across the coupling gap. This is measured as the difference in gap between the near and far sides of the coupling face as the shafts are rotated, expressed as thousandths of an inch per inch of coupling diameter (or mm per 100mm). It is corrected by raising or lowering one end of the machine (shimming the front feet vs rear feet differently) or by pivoting the machine laterally.
  • Generates a vibration at 1x rotational frequency in the axial direction, which helps distinguish it from parallel misalignment
  • Causes the coupling to flex once per revolution as the gap opens and closes
  • In a flanged coupling, angular misalignment causes the bolts on one side to be in tension while the other side is loose. Check flange bolt torque as an indicator.
  • Angular misalignment is often present alongside parallel misalignment. Correcting one without the other is common and leaves the machine still misaligned.
  • When shimming to correct angular misalignment, moving one end of the machine affects the other end. Work iteratively and check both ends after each adjustment.
Axial misalignment: shafts too close together (or too far apart)
Axial spacing refers to the gap between the two shaft ends across the coupling. Most flexible couplings require a specific gap, called the DBSE (distance between shaft ends), to function correctly. Too little gap means the shaft ends or coupling hubs can contact under thermal expansion or axial load. Too much gap means the coupling flexible element is stretched beyond its design range. Either condition causes abnormal loading and accelerated wear.
  • DBSE is specified by the coupling manufacturer. Measure and confirm before final installation.
  • Thermal expansion: shafts grow toward each other at operating temperature. Account for this when setting the cold gap.
  • Jaw couplings with a spider element have a specific recommended gap range. Consult the manufacturer data sheet.
  • Axial load from incorrect gap is transferred directly to the bearing thrust faces. This is a common undiagnosed cause of thrust bearing failure in pump applications.
  • On equipment with fixed axial positions (some gearboxes, motors with thrust bearings), adjusting DBSE requires moving one machine. Confirm which machine is the reference before adjusting.
Combined misalignment: parallel offset and angular simultaneously (most common in practice)
In practice, pure parallel or pure angular misalignment is rare. Most misaligned machines have both conditions present simultaneously. This is important because correcting one type of misalignment affects the other. Shimming to correct angular misalignment changes the parallel offset, and vice versa. The correct approach is to address both simultaneously through an iterative process, checking and adjusting until both conditions are within tolerance at the same time.
  • Always correct soft foot before attempting to correct parallel or angular misalignment. Soft foot makes alignment readings unstable and corrections unpredictable.
  • The standard sequence is: soft foot first, then rough alignment by eye, then fine alignment with instruments, then verify after coupling bolts are torqued.
  • A laser alignment system solves both parallel and angular simultaneously and shows the correction required at each foot in real time.
  • Even with a laser tool, always recheck after tightening coupling bolts and connecting piping. Pipe strain can pull a machine out of alignment after the tool is removed.
Alignment methods: from basic to precision
Straightedge and feeler gauge
The most basic alignment check. A straightedge laid across the two coupling hubs shows gross parallel misalignment. Feeler gauges measure the gap across the coupling face to indicate angular misalignment. This method is useful for rough alignment during initial installation or to confirm that a machine is not grossly out before applying instruments. It cannot achieve precision alignment and should not be relied on as a final check for anything other than the lowest-speed, most forgiving applications.
  • Useful for: initial rough alignment, quick field check, confirming gross misalignment before pulling a machine
  • Not suitable for: final alignment on anything running above roughly 1000 RPM, or any application with close coupling tolerances
  • Cannot account for soft foot, shaft sag, or thermal growth
Dial indicator: rim and face method
A dial indicator (or two) mounted on one shaft, reading off the rim and face of the other coupling hub. As the shafts are rotated together, the indicator records the total indicated runout (TIR) in both the radial (parallel) and axial (angular) directions. The readings are then used to calculate the required corrections at each machine foot. This is the traditional precision alignment method and is still widely used. It requires understanding of the math involved and careful attention to indicator setup to produce reliable results.
  • Useful for: precision alignment where laser equipment is not available, verifying laser alignment results, shaft runout measurement
  • Requires: accurate indicator mounting with no sag in the indicator bracket, rotating both shafts together, and correct application of the correction math
  • Bracket sag is a common error: the weight of the bracket deflects the indicator reading. Measure bracket sag and apply as a correction factor.
  • Takes more time and skill than laser alignment but produces equivalent results when done correctly
Laser alignment
A laser alignment system uses two sensor heads, one mounted on each shaft, that communicate wirelessly and calculate the exact position of each shaft relative to the other in real time. The system displays the misalignment condition and the exact shim changes and lateral moves required at each foot. Modern laser tools compensate automatically for bracket sag, thermal growth, and shaft sag. They are faster, more accurate, and easier to use correctly than dial indicators, and the real-time feedback makes iterative correction straightforward.
  • Useful for: all precision alignment work, machines with thermal growth requirements, high-speed applications, and any situation where documentation of alignment condition is required
  • Still requires correct soft foot correction before use
  • The tool is only as good as the setup. Loose sensor mounts, contaminated shaft surfaces, and shaft endplay all affect accuracy.
  • Most systems produce a printable alignment report, which is valuable for maintenance records and for comparing alignment condition over time
  • Common brands: Pruftechnik ROTALIGN, SKF TKSA series, Fluke 830, Fixturlaser
Field tip: the laser checks the laserA laser alignment system gives an answer to the micron and it is only as good as the dimensions typed into it and the brackets clamped to the shafts. Measure the coupling-to-feet distances with a tape, not from memory, and tap the brackets. When the laser says something that does not match what your eyes see at the coupling, believe your eyes and find the input error.
Alignment procedure: correct sequence is everything
Soft foot: always first, no exceptions
Soft foot is a condition where one or more of the machine feet does not sit flat and stable on the base. When a foot bolt is tightened, it distorts the machine frame, which shifts the shaft position. Attempting precision alignment on a machine with soft foot is a waste of time. The readings will be inconsistent and the corrections will not hold. Always check and correct soft foot before any alignment work. Loosen all foot bolts and retighten one at a time while monitoring the shaft position with a dial indicator. Any foot that causes the shaft to move when its bolt is tightened has soft foot and requires shimming to correct.
  • Acceptable soft foot: less than 0.002 inches (0.05mm) of shaft movement when any single foot bolt is tightened or loosened
  • Angular soft foot (one corner of the foot, not the whole foot) is harder to detect and correct than parallel soft foot
  • Never use more than three to four shims under any foot. Excessive shimming stacks create instability. If more correction is needed, machine a new base surface.
  • Recheck soft foot after every significant base or grout repair
Overview: the alignment sequence
01
Correct soft foot
As described above. Do not proceed until soft foot is within tolerance at all feet.
02
Rough alignment by eye or straightedge
Get the machine close before applying instruments. A grossly misaligned machine wastes instrument time and can exceed the range of the measurement tool. Use a straightedge across the coupling hubs to get parallel misalignment within approximately 1mm. Set DBSE (coupling gap) to the specified dimension.
03
Take alignment readings
With dial indicators or laser system, take readings in all four positions (top, bottom, left, right) for both parallel and angular conditions. Confirm readings are repeatable by taking two full sets and comparing.
04
Calculate and apply vertical corrections (shimming)
Vertical corrections are made by adding or removing shims under the machine feet. Calculate the required shim change at the front and rear feet independently. Angular misalignment requires different shim changes at each end. Parallel misalignment requires equal shim changes at both ends.
05
Apply horizontal corrections (lateral moves)
Move the machine laterally on its base using alignment jacking screws or a jack and drift. Lateral corrections follow the same logic as vertical corrections. Recheck soft foot after any lateral move.
06
Verify, torque coupling, and recheck
After corrections are within tolerance, torque all foot bolts to specification and recheck alignment. Coupling bolt torque and pipe connection can shift alignment. Always take a final reading with everything connected and torqued before signing off.

The rim and face method uses one or two dial indicators to measure both the parallel (radial) and angular (face) misalignment simultaneously. The indicator reading at the rim (side of the coupling hub) measures parallel offset. The indicator reading at the face (end of the coupling hub) measures angular misalignment.

01
Mount the indicator bracket on the driver shaft (motor). One indicator reads the rim of the driven coupling hub (pump side). The second reads the face. Ensure the bracket has no detectable sag. Measure sag by mounting the bracket horizontally and rotating 180 degrees. Half the difference between top and bottom readings is the sag correction value.
02
Zero both indicators at the 12 o'clock position. Rotate both shafts together (they must rotate as one unit) to 3, 6, and 9 o'clock. Record the rim and face readings at each position. A full set of four readings (12, 3, 6, 9) gives you the complete picture.
03
Apply sag correction to rim readings. Subtract the sag value from the 6 o'clock rim reading before doing the math.
04
Calculate vertical parallel offset (shimming required):
Vertical offset = (Rim reading at 6 o'clock: sag correction) / 2
Positive value = pump shaft is HIGH relative to motor shaft
Negative value = pump shaft is LOW: add shims
05
Calculate angular misalignment from face readings:
Angular gap difference = Face reading at 6 o'clock (with sign)
Shim correction at rear feet = (Gap difference / Coupling diameter) × Distance from coupling to rear feet
Shim correction at front feet = (Gap difference / Coupling diameter) × Distance from coupling to front feet
A positive face reading at 6 o'clock means the gap is wider at the bottom: the rear of the motor needs to come UP (add shims at rear feet).
06
Horizontal corrections follow identical math using the 3 and 9 o'clock readings for rim (parallel) and face (angular). Positive at 3 o'clock means the pump shaft is to the right of the motor shaft centreline.
07
Typical acceptable tolerances for dial indicator alignment:
Parallel offset: ≤ 0.002" TIR (0.05mm) for most industrial applications
Angular: ≤ 0.001" per inch of coupling diameter
High-speed (>3600 RPM): tighten both to ≤ 0.001" TIR
Always defer to the coupling manufacturer's published tolerance

These calculations assume a standard two-indicator rim-and-face setup. Reverse dial indicator setups use different math. If you are using a reverse dial arrangement, the correction formulas change. Consult a dedicated alignment reference or your laser system's manual for the reverse dial procedure.

Field tip: rough it in by eye before the indicators go onA machine that is 2 mm out will run the indicators off their range and waste an hour. Straightedge across the hubs, feeler in the gap, move it until it looks right, then set the indicators. The precision tools are for the last half millimetre.
Installation: fit, keyways, and assembly sequence
Coupling installation is where alignment problems are created or prevented. A hub that is not fully seated on the shaft, a keyway with excessive clearance, or a flexible element installed incorrectly will produce misalignment that no amount of machine adjustment can fully correct. Get the installation right before touching the alignment instruments.
01
Inspect shaft and hub bore
Check shaft diameter against hub bore specification. An interference fit hub requires heating or pressing. A clearance fit hub slides on but must be secured by a keyway and set screw or locking collar. Measure shaft runout at the coupling seat before installing. Maximum acceptable TIR for most applications is 0.001 to 0.002 inches. Runout at the shaft transfers directly to the coupling as apparent misalignment.
02
Keyway fit
The key should be a snug sliding fit in the shaft keyway and a light interference fit in the hub keyway. A loose key allows the hub to rock on the shaft, generating fretting and apparent misalignment under load. A key that is too tight can crack the hub during installation. Never use oversized keys as a repair for a worn keyway. Machine the keyway to the next standard size or sleeve the shaft.
03
Hub installation: support the shaft
When pressing or driving a coupling hub onto a shaft, the axial force must be reacted at the shaft, not transmitted through the bearings. Support the far end of the shaft against the installation force. Pressing a hub onto a shaft without supporting the far end drives the force through the bearing, causing brinelling of the races. Use a hub installation tool or a threaded draw bolt through the shaft end where geometry allows.
04
Set DBSE before final hub seating
Confirm the distance between shaft ends matches the coupling manufacturer's specified DBSE before torquing hubs to final position. Once both hubs are fully seated, DBSE can only be changed by repositioning a machine. Measure DBSE with a vernier caliper or rule and compare to the data sheet value.
05
Install flexible element last
On jaw, disc, and grid couplings, install the flexible element after both hubs are seated and the machine has been aligned. Installing the element before alignment forces you to work against its resistance and can introduce pre-load into the element. On disc couplings, confirm the disc pack bolt pattern is oriented correctly per the manufacturer's instruction before torquing.
06
Torque sequence and final check
Torque all coupling bolts in a star pattern to the manufacturer's specification. Under-torqued coupling bolts back off under vibration and create fretting at the interface. Over-torqued bolts stretch and lose clamping force. After torquing, rotate the assembly by hand one full revolution and feel for any tight spots or resistance changes. Any resistance that was not present before the coupling was assembled indicates a problem: recheck hub seating, flexible element condition, and DBSE.
After connection: pipe strain check
One of the most commonly skipped steps in pump and compressor installation is checking alignment after connecting the process piping. Pipe strain (stress in the connected piping pulling or pushing on the machine nozzles) can shift a perfectly aligned machine by several thousandths of an inch. The check is simple: take alignment readings with piping disconnected, then connect the piping and take readings again. Any change greater than 0.001 to 0.002 inches indicates pipe strain that must be corrected at the piping, not by realigning the machine.
  • Pipe strain is most common on large bore, rigid piping systems and on high-temperature lines that grow significantly during operation
  • The correct fix is to add pipe supports, expansion loops, or flexible connections to relieve the strain at the nozzle
  • Never force piping into alignment with the machine nozzle. The strain goes somewhere, and that somewhere is the machine bearings and casing.
Alignment fault diagnosis

Every alignment that will not come in has one of five causes: invalid readings (sag, a loose bracket, axial float), soft foot, pipe strain, the arithmetic, or thermal growth. Check them in that order. The validity check on rim readings (top plus bottom equals left plus right) is the first thing, because if it fails nothing after it means anything. Then soft foot on both machines. Then readings with the piping off and on. Then the sign convention and the dimensions. Then whether the machine runs hot.

A laser system removes the arithmetic and the sag and leaves every other cause in place.

Alignment safety: pinch points, moving machines, and the coupling gap
Alignment puts your hands on shafts, in the coupling gap, and under machine feet.
Every step of an alignment involves either turning a shaft by hand, reaching into a coupling to fit a bracket, or moving a motor on its base with a bar or jacking bolts. The machine is locked out and verified before the guard comes off, and it stays that way until the guard goes back on.
Lockout for alignment work
  • Both machines locked out and verified: the motor, and anything that can turn the driven machine (process flow, gravity, a windmilling fan, another drive on the same shaft).
  • The coupling guard comes off after lockout and goes back on before the lock. No exceptions for a quick reading.
  • Rotating shafts for readings: turn by the coupling hub or a bar in a designed spot, never with fingers between the hubs or in the coupling gap. A hand in the gap when the shaft turns is a crushed hand.
  • Remote and automatic starts: a machine on a PLC or a level control can start on its own. Isolation is at the source, not at the stop button.
  • Regulation 851, section 75 for lockout; sections 24 and 25 for guards.
Moving the machine
  • A motor on its base is heavy. Horizontal moves use jacking bolts or a bar with a fulcrum. Hands never between the motor foot and anything solid.
  • Vertical moves: the motor is supported on jacking bolts or on the loosened foot bolts, not on a hand or a shim being slid in. Shims go in and out with the foot bolt snugged on the other feet and the motor stable.
  • Never leave a machine sitting on jacking bolts alone. Foot bolts snug before hands go under it.
  • Large motors and pumps are lifted for base work. Rigging rules apply.
Tools and instruments
  • Dial indicator brackets clamp to the shaft or hub. A bracket that comes loose while the shaft is turned falls into the coupling. Check it before every rotation.
  • Laser alignment systems are Class 2 lasers: safe for brief exposure, but do not stare into the beam and do not point it at anyone.
  • Feeler gauges and straightedges in a running coupling: never. Rough alignment is done locked out like everything else.
  • Hot alignment checks on a running machine (thermal growth) use non-contact methods or are done immediately after shutdown with the machine locked. A hot casing burns.
Restart
  • Guard on, all tools and shims accounted for, foot bolts torqued, alignment readings recorded.
  • First start with everyone clear of the coupling and the guard. Listen and watch before anyone approaches.
  • Recheck after torque and after the first run. Then the lock comes off the paperwork too.
Field tip: count the shimsEvery shim that goes under a foot is written down. Every shim that comes out is written down. At the end the count matches. A shim left on the base finds its way under the next motor, and a shim in the coupling guard finds its way into the coupling.
