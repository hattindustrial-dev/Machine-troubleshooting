Root Cause
Module 16
Every prevent-recurrence line in this series points here
Two hundred and sixty diagnostic results across fifteen modules end with what to change so the failure does not come back. That change is only right if the cause is right, and finding the cause is a skill separate from fixing the fault. A parts-swapper replaces the bearing. A troubleshooter finds out why it failed. A root cause analyst finds out why the plant let it fail, and changes that. This module is the third skill.
Three levels of cause
LevelWhat it answersBearing exampleFix at this level
Physical causeWhat broke, and by what mechanismThe bearing overheated; grease churned; the cage crackedReplace the bearing. Fails again in six months.
Human causeWhat someone did or did not do that produced the physical causeThe bearing was filled with grease, relief plug in, every PMTell the technician. Fails again when they are on vacation.
Latent (system) causeWhat in the plant's systems made the human action normalThe PM sheet says "grease" with no quantity and no procedure; nobody owns the lubrication programWrite the quantity on the sheet. Assign the program. Fails no more.
  • Stop at the physical cause and you have a repair. Stop at the human cause and you have a blame. Reach the latent cause and you have a fix.
  • Most latent causes are boring: a missing number on a sheet, a spare that was never stocked, a drawing that was never updated, a task nobody owns. That is why they persist.
  • Not every failure deserves a full analysis. A repeat failure, an expensive one, a near miss, or one that hurt someone does. A first-time failure of a cheap part gets read (Reading Failed Parts tab) and logged, and becomes a full analysis if it happens again.
Field tip: the second time is the first time you can knowOne failure is a data point. Two is a pattern. When a part fails on the same machine for the second time, the work order should say "repeat" and the analysis should start before the new part goes in. A plant that tracks repeat failures by machine finds that a small number of machines produce most of the work, and that is where the analysis pays.
The method: eight steps in order
  • 1. Preserve the evidence. Before anyone cleans, disassembles, or throws anything away. Photographs in place. Oil sample. The failed part bagged and labelled. The control system alarm log saved. Evidence tab.
  • 2. Define the problem in one sentence. What failed, on which machine, when, and what it cost (downtime, injury, scrap). "The head pulley bearing on conveyor C-12 seized on 4 September, stopping the line for six hours." Not "bearing problems on C-12."
  • 3. Build the timeline. Every event leading up to the failure, with times: the last PM, the last repair, the last product change, the last alarm, what the operator noticed, what maintenance did. Work orders, logs, and interviews. Most causes are visible in the timeline before any analysis.
  • 4. Collect the data. Readings (vibration, temperature, amps, oil analysis) before and at failure. The failed part read (Reading Failed Parts tab). The drawings, the PM sheet, the procedure, the spare's specification.
  • 5. Find the physical cause. The mechanism: fatigue, overload, wear, corrosion, lubrication failure, contamination, misalignment. The part tells you this if it was preserved and read.
  • 6. Ask why until the answer is a system. Five Whys, or a fishbone if the causes are many. Keep going past the person to the process that shaped what they did.
  • 7. Decide the corrective actions. One for the physical cause (the repair), one or more for the latent cause (the change). Each with an owner and a date. A corrective action without an owner is a wish.
  • 8. Verify. After the change, did the failure stop. Set a date to check, check it, and close the analysis only when the evidence says it worked.
Who is in the room
  • The person who found it, the person who fixed it, the operator who runs it, and whoever owns the PM program. Four people, an hour, at the machine if possible.
  • Not a blame meeting. The moment it becomes one, people stop telling you what happened, and the analysis is worthless. The question is always "what about the system made that the normal thing to do."
  • The engineer or vendor when the physical cause is beyond the plant's ability to read: a metallurgical failure, a design question, a repeated failure of a new part.
Evidence: the part is only readable once
The first person to touch a failed part decides whether anyone will ever know why it failed
A bearing that is wiped clean, hammered off the shaft, and dropped in the scrap bin has been erased. A seal face that has been handled with greasy fingers cannot be read. A fracture face that has been rubbed together, or has rusted for a week in a puddle, has lost the marks that point to the origin. Preserve first. Analyse later. The cost of keeping a part for a week is nothing; the cost of losing the evidence is doing the whole failure again.
In place, before disassembly
  • Photographs from several angles, with something for scale, before anything is touched. The position of parts, the debris pattern, the oil trail, the heat discolouration, the wear track on the shaft, the shim stack, the belt position on the pulley.
  • Readings: temperature by infrared while it is still warm, the vibration reading if there is one, the amps on the panel, the take-up position, the sight glass level.
  • The control system: alarm history, trend data, the last hour of the drive log. Save it before it rolls off.
  • Witnesses: what the operator saw, heard, and smelled, and when, in their words, written down that day.
During and after disassembly
  • Disassemble gently. A puller, not a hammer. Mark orientation (which way was up, which end was drive) with a paint pen before parts come apart.
  • Do not clean the part. Do not wire brush a fracture face, degrease a bearing, or wipe a seal face. Loose debris is bagged separately. The dirt is evidence.
  • Do not fit the two halves of a fracture back together. Rubbing them destroys the surface that shows where the crack started.
  • Bag, label (machine, position, date, who), and store dry. Fracture faces coated in light oil or sealed in a bag with a desiccant if they will not be read for a while.
  • Oil and grease samples from the failed component, in a clean labelled bottle, before anything is flushed.
  • Keep the spare's paperwork: the part number fitted, its source, its date. A wrong part is a common cause and the only evidence is the box.
Field tip: a shoebox and a markerEvery failed part that comes off a machine goes in a box with the work order number and the date on it, and the box sits on a shelf for a month. Most get thrown out. The one that turns out to be the second failure on that machine is sitting there, unclean, unhammered, readable. That shelf is the cheapest reliability program in the plant.

Reading a fracture face: finding the origin
  • Beach marks (curved lines like a shoreline) are the growth of a fatigue crack; they curve around the origin. Follow the curves back to where they are tightest: that is where the crack started. The origin is where the cause is (a stress raiser, a nick, a corrosion pit, a keyway corner).
  • Chevron marks (V shapes) on a brittle fracture point back toward the origin like arrows.
  • Ratchet marks (small steps around the edge of a fracture) are multiple crack origins that joined; many ratchet marks mean high stress or many stress raisers all round, like a shaft with a sharp shoulder.
  • The final fracture zone (rough, crystalline, the part that let go at the end) tells you the load: a small final zone means the crack grew slowly under a low load; a large one means the part was heavily loaded and failed early in the crack's life.
  • Beach marks that are smooth and rubbed: the crack faces were pressed together during growth (bending, rotating bending). Rotating bending fatigue on a shaft starts at the surface and the final zone is off centre in the direction of the load.
  • Torsional fatigue on a shaft: a 45 degree helical fracture, or a star pattern from a keyway.
Field tip: the origin is where the fix isFind the origin of the crack and you have found the stress raiser: the keyway with a sharp corner, the shoulder with no radius, the corrosion pit, the tool mark, the weld toe. The part did not fail because it was weak. It failed because something concentrated the stress at that spot, and the new part has the same spot.
Five Whys and the fishbone
Five Whys
Start from the physical cause and ask why it happened. Then ask why that happened. Keep asking until the answer is a system, a process, or a decision, and not a person or a part. Five is a guide; some chains need three and some need seven. Each answer must be a fact you can check, not a guess.
Problem: motor DE bearing seized, line down 6 hours
Why? The bearing overheated and the cage collapsed. (Read the bearing: grease churned, cage cracked, no contamination.)
Why? It was overfilled with grease. (Grease found in the windings and past the inner cap.)
Why? The technician pumped until grease appeared at the relief, with the plug in. (Interview.)
Why? The PM sheet says "grease motor bearings" with no quantity and no procedure. (The sheet.)
Why? The PM program was written from the old CMMS import and nobody has owned lubrication since the last reliability tech left. (The org chart.)

Corrective actions:
Physical: replace bearing, clean windings.
Latent: quantity on every grease PM by formula; relief plug step in the procedure; lubrication program assigned to a named owner. Verify: no repeat in 12 months, grease consumption tracked.
  • Each why is answered with evidence, not opinion. "Because the technician was careless" is an opinion and it stops the chain. "Because the sheet gave no quantity" is a fact on a piece of paper.
  • A chain that ends at a person has not ended. Ask what made that the normal thing for that person to do.
  • Two chains are often needed: why did it fail, and why was it not caught before it failed (the detection chain: the PM did not include a temperature check, the vibration route did not cover this motor).
Fishbone (cause and effect, six categories)
When there are many possible causes and one chain is not enough, list every candidate under six headings and then test each one against the evidence. The ones the evidence supports get a Five Whys chain. The ones it rules out get crossed off and stay on the diagram so the next person knows they were considered.

Traps: how analyses go wrong
  • Blaming the part. "Bad bearing." Bearings from a reputable supplier are almost never defective. A part that fails was installed, lubricated, loaded, or aligned into failing. Read it.
  • Stopping at the first cause. The bearing was overgreased. Why? That is the analysis. Stopping here fixes one bearing.
  • "Operator error" or "technician error" as a root cause. It is a description of the human cause and it is never the root. The system that made the error easy or normal is the root.
  • Confusing symptom, cause, and contributing factor. Vibration is a symptom. Misalignment is a cause. A worn coupling that absorbed the misalignment for two years is a contributing factor. They get different actions.
  • Confirmation bias. The first theory becomes the conclusion and the evidence is read to fit it. Write down two alternative theories before looking at the part, and try to disprove the favourite.
  • Evidence gone. The part was cleaned, the oil was flushed, the log rolled off. The analysis is now guesswork. Preserve first.
  • Analysis without action. A report that names the root cause and changes nothing. Every analysis ends with an owner and a date, or it was a conversation.
  • Action without verification. The PM sheet was changed; did the failure stop? Set a date and check.
  • Analysing everything. Full analysis on every failure exhausts everyone and nothing gets done. Repeat failures, expensive failures, and safety events get the full method. The rest get read and logged.
  • Fixing it with a spare. Adding a spare motor to the shelf treats the symptom (downtime) and leaves the cause. Sometimes the spare is the right call, but it is not a root cause fix and should not be recorded as one.
Field tip: write the cause as a sentence a stranger could check"Lubrication issue" is not a cause. "The DE bearing on M-114 was filled to the relief with the plug in at every quarterly PM because the PM task reads 'grease' with no quantity" is a cause, and anyone can walk over to the PM sheet and confirm it. If the cause cannot be written that way, it has not been found.

Which way to go with a repeat failure

The other fifteen modules find the fault. This tree takes a failure that has already been diagnosed and asks what kind of cause is behind it, so the corrective action lands at the right level. Start with the failed part in front of you and the last analysis (if any) in hand.

Self-check: one question at a time, tap an answer, read why
Questions are grouped by the tab they test. Get one wrong and the correct answer lights up green with a one-line reason; use the link to reopen the tab and read it again. For a training program, a pass is every section answered and the reasons read, not a percentage.
Safety during failure investigation
A failed machine is a machine in an unknown state
Something broke, and until you know what, you do not know what else is loose, cracked, pressurised, or about to let go. Evidence collection happens after isolation, not instead of it. Never restart a failed machine to see what happens. Never reach in to retrieve a part while anything can move. Never handle a fracture face with bare hands; fresh fracture edges cut.
  • Lockout and stored energy release before the guard comes off, same as any maintenance. A machine that stopped because a coupling sheared still has a motor that will run and a load that may be hanging on a broken part.
  • Broken parts are sharp. Gloves for handling; bare hands only for surfaces that need reading, and then by the edges.
  • Hot: a seized bearing, a slipped belt, a burnt motor. Infrared before touch.
  • Chemical: the oil, the process fluid, the grease, and anything the failure released. SDS and PPE.
  • Fragments: a burst wheel, a thrown coupling, a shattered seal face. Look for what is missing and where it went before assuming the area is clear.
  • Interviews are not interrogations. Someone who is afraid of blame will not tell you what happened, and what happened is the evidence.
