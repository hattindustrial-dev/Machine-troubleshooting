Fans and Blowers
Module 17
A fan is a pump for air, and the pump module's first rule applies
Fans create flow. The duct system resists that flow, and the resistance registers as pressure. A fan running against a closed damper is a centrifugal pump at shutoff: little flow, all the power going into heat and noise, and on some fan types, stall and surge. The fan curve, the system curve, the affinity laws, and the operating point are the same ideas as the pump module, with air instead of water and a much bigger, lighter wheel that is far more sensitive to balance and buildup.
What goes wrong with fans, in order of how often
  • Buildup on the wheel: product, dust, moisture, ice. The wheel goes out of balance, vibration rises, bearings fail. Most fan bearing failures are balance failures, and most balance failures are dirt.
  • Belt drive problems: tension, sheave wear, ratio changed by a replacement sheave. Power Transmission module.
  • Bearing failure from unbalance, over-tension, misalignment, heat on hot gas fans, or contamination. Bearing Failure module.
  • System changes: dampers, filters loading up, ducts added or blocked. The fan moves on its curve and draws different power, and people blame the fan.
  • Wheel damage: erosion, corrosion, a blade cracked at the hub weld, a wheel that has slipped on the shaft.
  • Stall and surge on backward-inclined and axial fans throttled too far.
Field tip: amps tell you where the fan is on its curveOn a backward-inclined or airfoil centrifugal fan, motor amps rise with flow: a fan with the discharge damper wide open and no duct on the outlet will overload the motor. On a forward-curved fan and an axial fan it is the opposite or nearly flat. Know which you have before you open a damper to "give it more air" and trip the motor. The fan laws tab has the curves.

  • The cube law is why a replacement sheave "close enough" trips a motor, and why a VFD slowing a fan 20 percent halves its power.
  • Density: pressure and power scale with air density. A fan rated for 20°C air moves the same volume of 200°C air but at 60 percent of the pressure and power; a cold start on a hot gas fan overloads the motor.
  • System curve: the duct resistance rises with the square of flow. Where the fan curve crosses the system curve is the operating point. Close a damper, the system curve steepens, the operating point moves left (less flow, more pressure). Open a duct, it moves right.
  • Backward-inclined and airfoil power curves peak and fall off at high flow (non-overloading). Forward-curved power keeps rising with flow (overloading). Axial power is highest at low flow, near stall.
Stall and surge
Backward-inclined centrifugal fans and all axial fans have a region on the curve, to the left of the peak pressure, where the airflow separates from the blades. The fan stalls: flow drops, pressure fluctuates, the fan pulses and roars, and the vibration can wreck the wheel and bearings. A system throttled too far (a damper closed down, filters loaded, a duct blocked) pushes the fan into it. Two fans in parallel where one is stronger can push the weaker into stall.
  • Sound: a low pulsing roar, and pressure gauges swinging.
  • Cure: open the system (dampers, filters), reduce the speed, or fit a stall-limiting device on axial fans. A fan selected too close to its peak pressure will live in stall.
  • Forward-curved and radial fans do not stall in the same way; they are used where the system varies widely.
The wheel: balance, buildup, and clearance
  • Balance: fan wheels are balanced to ISO 21940 (formerly 1940) grade G6.3 for general industrial, G2.5 for high speed. A fan is a large light rotor at high tip speed, and a few grams of product on one blade is a vibration problem. Buildup is removed before any balancing, or the balance job is balancing the dirt.
  • Buildup: sticky product, moisture with dust, ice, paint overspray, and corrosion products all accumulate unevenly. It comes off in chunks, which is when the vibration jumps. Cleaning access (a door in the housing) and a cleaning interval on the PM, sized by the product.
  • Wheel to inlet cone clearance: the overlap and the radial gap between the wheel inlet and the inlet cone set the fan's efficiency and pressure. A wheel that has moved on the shaft, a cone knocked out of position, or a bearing with axial play opens the gap and the fan loses pressure and draws different power. The manufacturer gives the dimension; check it after any wheel or bearing work.
  • Wheel on the shaft: hub set screws, a taper-lock bushing, or a key and locknut. A wheel that has slipped is out of position (clearance) and often out of balance. Match-mark the hub to the shaft.
  • Erosion and corrosion: blades thin from the leading edge, and the wheel loses balance and strength. A radial fan on abrasive dust is inspected for blade thickness. Cracks at the blade-to-backplate welds on centrifugal wheels are the failure that throws a blade; dye penetrant on the PM for high-speed wheels.
  • Wheel rotation direction: a centrifugal wheel run backward moves about half the air at much higher power draw; it does not run "in reverse," it runs badly. The arrow on the housing.
Field tip: clean it before you balance itThe most common fan vibration job starts with a balancing instrument and ends with a wire brush. Lock out, open the housing, look at the wheel. If one side has product on it, that is the unbalance. Clean the whole wheel, close up, run it, read the vibration, and then decide whether it needs balancing. Balancing a dirty wheel puts weight on to cancel the dirt, and when the dirt comes off, the weight is the unbalance.
Bearings and drives: fan-specific points
  • Arrangement: fans are built in numbered arrangements (AMCA). Arrangement 1 is an overhung wheel on a shaft between two bearings, belt driven; arrangement 4 is the wheel on the motor shaft; arrangement 8 is direct coupled with its own bearings; arrangement 9 is belt driven with the motor on the side. The arrangement tells you where the bearings are, what loads they see, and how the wheel comes off.
  • Overhung wheels (arrangement 1, 9) put a bending load on the shaft and the inboard bearing carries most of it plus the belt pull. That bearing is the one that fails.
  • Belt drives on fans: tension by gauge, sheave gauge, and ratio checked against the drawing after any sheave change. The cube law makes a ratio error expensive. Power Transmission module.
  • Hot gas fans: the shaft conducts heat to the bearings. A heat slinger (a small disc on the shaft between the housing and the bearing) throws air over the shaft; a cooling wheel; a shaft seal on the housing. Bearing grease rated for the temperature, and the interval shortened. A hot gas fan started cold overloads the motor (denser air) until the gas heats.
  • Fan bearings are often pillow blocks: fixing ring on the drive side, the other free to float for thermal growth, per the Bearing Failure module. A fan shaft that heats and cannot grow bends.
  • Large fans with long shafts have a critical speed; run-up through it must be quick, and a VFD must not park the fan on it. Vibration module, resonance.
  • Backdraft dampers and outlet dampers: a fan started against a closed outlet damper on a forward-curved unit is at minimum power, which is the design intent; on a backward-inclined unit it is near stall. Start-up damper position is on the procedure.
  • Windmilling: a fan in a duct with draft turns with the motor off. Block it before the guard comes off; it is also a generator on the motor terminals.
Fan fault diagnosis

Running: amps against nameplate and against last time. Damper positions. Filter differential pressure. Static pressure at the fan if there is a gauge. Vibration by hand or by meter at both bearings. Belt noise. Infrared on bearings and motor.

Locked out, blocked against windmilling: open the housing. Wheel buildup, blade condition, cracks at the hub, wheel position and cone clearance, rotation arrow. Shaft play at the bearings. Belt tension and sheave gauge. Sheave diameters against the drawing.

Then think about the system. A fan that changed behaviour with no fan fault has a system that changed: a damper, a filter, a duct, a process.

Fan safety: stored energy in a big light wheel, and ducts
A fan wheel stores more energy than it looks like, and it keeps turning
A large wheel at speed carries enough kinetic energy to take a hand off, and it coasts for minutes after the motor stops. A fan in a duct with draft turns with the power off, indefinitely. Lock out the motor, wait for or brake the coast-down, and block the wheel against windmilling before any guard or access door is opened.
  • Inlet and outlet guards on any fan with an open inlet or outlet within reach. An open inlet on a large fan will draw in and hold a person.
  • Windmilling: a wheel turning from draft is a hazard and an electrical generator. Block the wheel with a designed device or a bar in a spoke, not a hand on the wheel.
  • Ducts and plenums are confined spaces when they meet the definition. Entry procedure, atmosphere test, isolation of every fan that could move air through it.
  • Hot gas fans: housing, wheel, and gas are at process temperature. Cool down and isolate the gas source before opening.
  • A wheel that has thrown a blade or cracked at the hub is a wheel that will throw the next one. Do not run it to "see if it holds."
  • Belt drive guards, Regulation 851 sections 24 and 25. The fan drive is the belt drive most likely to be found with the guard off, because it is on the roof.
  • Dust: a fan handling combustible dust is part of a dust explosion system. Housekeeping, bonding, and no ignition sources (a rubbing wheel is one).
