Reading a bearing number
A standard bearing designation says what it is and how big it is. The first digit or digits are the type, the next the size series, and the last two are the bore code: multiply by five for the bore in millimetres from 04 upward (00 is 10 mm, 01 is 12, 02 is 15, 03 is 17). Suffixes carry the details that matter for replacement.
ExampleMeaning
62056 = deep groove ball; 2 = light series; 05 = 25 mm bore. Common on small motors and pumps.
6309 C3Deep groove ball, medium series, 45 mm bore, C3 (extra) internal clearance. The motor bearing.
NU 210N = cylindrical roller; U = no ribs on the inner ring (free axial float); 2 = light series; 10 = 50 mm bore.
22220 E2 = spherical roller; 22 = width series; 20 = 100 mm bore; E = high capacity design. Pillow blocks and fans.
7208 B7 = angular contact ball; 2 = light; 08 = 40 mm bore; B = 40 degree contact angle. Thrust capacity.
322103 = tapered roller (metric series); 22 = width and angle series; 10 = 50 mm bore. Set in pairs with preload or endplay.
Suffixes: 2RS, 2Z, ZZTwo rubber seals; two metal shields; two shields (maker dependent). Sealed for life, no regreasing.
Suffixes: C3, C4, CNInternal clearance greater than normal, much greater, normal. Reference module has the values.
Suffixes: J, M, TNCage material: pressed steel, machined brass, polyamide. Brass for heat and shock; polyamide for speed and quiet; note polyamide has a temperature limit.
Prefixes and inch bearingsInch tapered rollers use cone and cup numbers (for example LM11949 and LM11910) that do not encode size; look them up. Pillow block inserts (UC, SA) encode the shaft size in the last two digits the same way.
  • Order by the full number including suffixes. A 6309 and a 6309 C3 are the same size and different bearings.
  • The number is stamped on the ring face. Read it before the bearing is cleaned and, if it is worn off, measure bore, OD, and width and match all three.
  • Interchange between makers is by the ISO designation; suffix letters differ by maker for the same feature.
Guided fault diagnosis
Field tip: two bearings, same shaft, compare themInfrared on both bearings of the same shaft, and a screwdriver on each housing. They see the same speed and the same lubricant; the one that is hotter or louder is the one with the problem, and the difference between them is a cleaner signal than either number alone. The same trick works across two identical machines side by side.
Lubrication: the most common cause of bearing failure
The majority of rolling element bearing failures trace back to lubrication problems, and the most common of those is overgreasing, not undergreasing. More grease is not better. The rolling elements need to run through a film, not a reservoir. Understanding what the lubricant is actually doing inside the bearing changes how you approach every PM interval.
Grease lubrication
Grease is the most common lubricant for rolling element bearings in industrial equipment. It provides a semi-solid film between rolling elements and races, seals against contaminants, and stays in place without a circulating system. The grease in a bearing is not consumed the way oil is. It is sheared, oxidized, and contaminated over time until it can no longer provide adequate film thickness.
  • Grease quantity: most bearings should be filled to 30 to 50 percent of free space in the housing. Full packing causes churning and heat.
  • Regreasing: apply only enough to replenish degraded grease, not to pack the housing. A grease relief plug or purge fitting should be open during regreasing to allow old grease to exit.
  • Grease compatibility: mixing incompatible grease types (lithium vs calcium vs polyurea) can cause the lubricant to soften, harden, or separate. When changing grease type, purge the bearing completely first.
  • Temperature matters: grease has an operating temperature range. Exceeding it causes the thickener to break down and the oil to bleed out. Check grease specification against application temperature.
  • Grease colour is not a reliable indicator of condition. Black or dark grease is not automatically bad. Use intervals, temperature trends, and appearance together.
Oil lubrication
Oil lubrication is used where grease cannot adequately manage heat, where continuous flushing of contaminants is needed, or where bearing speeds are too high for grease. Oil splash, bath, and circulating systems each have different maintenance requirements. Oil condition is easier to monitor than grease condition, which is one of its practical advantages.
  • Oil level in splash or bath lubrication: too low means inadequate coverage, too high means churning and heat. Confirm the correct level marking for the specific housing design.
  • Oil change intervals are based on operating temperature and contamination, not just time. High-temperature applications need more frequent changes.
  • Milky oil indicates water ingress. Identify and eliminate the source before refilling. Water in bearing oil destroys the lubricating film rapidly.
  • On circulating systems, check filter condition and flow rate regularly. A blocked filter bypasses and sends unfiltered oil to the bearings.
Overgreasing: the most misunderstood PM task
Overgreasing is responsible for more bearing failures than undergreasing in most industrial environments. When a bearing housing is packed too full, the rolling elements are forced to churn through the excess grease. This generates heat, which accelerates oxidation of the lubricant, which accelerates bearing wear. The bearing runs hot, the grease darkens, the interval gets shortened, more grease is added, and the cycle continues.
  • Signs of overgreasing: bearing housing is hot to the touch, grease purging out of seals or relief plugs, bearing noise that clears after a short run-in period as excess grease is displaced.
  • Motor bearings are particularly vulnerable because motors are often regreased by non-mechanical personnel following a "when in doubt, grease it" approach.
  • The correct amount is a measured addition, not a full purge. Know the bearing housing volume and apply the calculated quantity.
  • After regreasing, it is normal for bearing temperature to rise slightly as excess grease is worked through and displaced. Monitor for 30 to 60 minutes. Temperature should return to baseline.
  • If a bearing consistently runs hot after regreasing, suspect overgreasing before condemning the bearing.
Intervals and condition monitoring
Greasing intervals should be based on bearing size, speed, temperature, and environment, not on a fixed calendar schedule applied uniformly across all equipment. A conveyor idler bearing in a dusty environment needs more frequent attention than a low-speed fan bearing in a clean room.
  • SKF, NSK, and other bearing manufacturers publish regreasing quantity and interval calculators. Use them as a starting point, then adjust based on observed condition.
  • Trending bearing temperature over time is one of the most reliable early indicators of lubrication degradation. A gradual rise in baseline temperature warrants inspection.
  • Ultrasonic lubrication monitoring allows greasing while running: add grease until the ultrasonic signal drops to a baseline level, then stop. This removes the guesswork from quantity.
  • Document what grease type, quantity, and date were applied. This information is essential when investigating a failure.
Installation: most bearing failures begin here
A bearing that is correctly specified and correctly installed in a correctly aligned machine will last its rated service life under normal operating conditions. The majority of premature bearing failures are installation failures, not product failures. Heat damage from improper fitting, contamination during handling, and incorrect preload are all preventable at installation.
Fixing rings: drive side only
On pillow block bearing assemblies with two housings on a common shaft, the fixing ring (locking collar or adapter) must be applied to the drive side bearing only. The non-drive side bearing must be left free to float axially. This allows the shaft to expand thermally during operation without generating axial load against the bearing races. If both bearings are fixed, thermal expansion has nowhere to go. The shaft will push against one or both bearings, generating axial load the bearing was not designed to carry. The result is accelerated wear, heat, and eventual failure that looks like a lubrication problem or overload but is actually a constraint problem created at installation.
  • Drive side: fixing ring engaged, bearing locked to shaft
  • Non-drive side: fixing ring disengaged or absent, bearing free to slide axially in housing
  • When in doubt about which end is the drive side, follow the power: the drive side is the end closest to the coupling or sprocket
  • This rule applies to standard pillow block and flanged cartridge arrangements. Specific bearing types (angular contact, thrust bearings) have their own preload and constraint requirements
Installation procedure: rolling element bearings
01
Inspect before installing
Check the new bearing for damage from shipping or storage. Bearings should be stored horizontally in original packaging until the moment of installation. A bearing that has been stored vertically on a shelf for months may have brinelled (false brinelling from vibration) race surfaces before it ever sees a shaft.
02
Clean the shaft and housing bore
Remove all old lubricant, corrosion, and debris from the shaft seat and housing bore. Check shaft diameter and housing bore against the bearing specification. An undersized shaft causes the inner race to spin on the shaft (fretting, black powder at the bore). An oversized shaft can crack the inner race during installation.
03
Apply force to the correct ring only
When pressing a bearing onto a shaft, all force must be applied to the inner race. When pressing into a housing, all force must be applied to the outer race. Never drive force through the rolling elements. This is the most common installation error and it causes brinelling of the races immediately. Use a bearing installation tool, a sleeve of the correct diameter, or an induction heater for interference fits.
04
Heat for interference fits: induction heater preferred
For bearings with an interference fit on the shaft, heating the inner race allows it to expand for installation without force. An induction heater is the correct tool. A temperature of 80 to 100 degrees Celsius above ambient is typically sufficient. Do not exceed 120 degrees Celsius or you risk altering the metallurgy of the bearing. Never use an open flame to heat a bearing. Oil bath heating is acceptable if the oil temperature is controlled and measured.
05
Check alignment before first run
A newly installed bearing does not correct misalignment. Confirm shaft alignment to the driver before energizing. Rotate the assembly by hand after installation and feel for smooth, even resistance with no rough spots or tight points. Any roughness at this stage is an installation problem, not a bearing defect.
06
Initial lubrication and run-in
Apply the correct quantity of grease before first run. After startup, monitor bearing temperature for the first 30 to 60 minutes. A new bearing will run slightly warmer initially as grease distributes. Temperature should stabilize and then hold steady. If temperature continues to rise after the first hour of operation, shut down and investigate before damage progresses.
Sleeve and journal bearing installation
Sleeve and journal bearings (plain bearings, babbitt bearings, bronze bushings) operate on a hydrodynamic oil film rather than rolling elements. As the shaft rotates, it draws oil into a converging gap between the journal and bearing surface, building pressure that lifts the shaft clear of the bearing material. This pressurized film is called the oil wedge, and it is the entire basis of how the bearing functions. Installation requires different considerations from rolling element bearings. The clearance between the journal (shaft) and the bearing surface is critical. Too tight prevents the oil wedge from forming correctly, causing overheating and seizure. Too loose allows the shaft to move within the bearing, causing vibration, oil whirl, and impact loading that degrades the bearing surface.
  • Clearance specification is typically expressed as a ratio of journal diameter. Consult the equipment manual for the specific value.
  • Babbitt bearings that are being replaced should be inspected for the failure mode before discarding: wiping (overload or oil starvation), cavitation erosion (oil supply issue), fatigue cracking (cyclic overload), or corrosion (water or acid contamination in the oil).
  • Oil supply holes and grooves must align correctly with the housing feed ports after installation. A sleeve installed 180 degrees out of orientation blocks the oil supply entirely.
  • Run-in is critical for sleeve bearings. Follow the equipment manufacturer's run-in procedure. Startup without adequate oil pressure is the most common cause of new sleeve bearing failure.
Field tip: the bearing stays in the boxThe bearing comes out of its packaging at the moment it goes onto the shaft, not before. A bearing sitting open on the bench for an hour has collected the grinding dust in the air, and that dust goes into the raceways. The packaging is clean; the shop is not.

Bearing work safety: heat, force, and pressure
Bearing work uses three things that hurt people: heat, force, and stored pressure.
Induction heaters and oil baths put bearings at 110 to 120°C. Pullers and presses put tonnes of force into a component that can let go without warning. Grease guns generate enough pressure to inject grease through skin. None of this is exotic, and all of it goes wrong when the job is rushed.
Heating bearings
  • Induction heaters: strong magnetic field. Pacemakers and implanted devices stay clear. Nothing metallic in the field but the bearing and the yoke. Demagnetise the bearing after heating (most heaters do it automatically; confirm it did).
  • Temperature limit 120°C for standard bearings. Above that the steel loses hardness and the seals and cages are damaged. Use the heater's probe, not a guess.
  • Oil bath: oil at 110°C is a burn and a fire. Thermostat, lid, and a rack so the bearing is not on the bottom of the pan.
  • A heated bearing is handled with heat-resistant gloves and goes onto the shaft in one motion. Once it starts to cool it seizes where it is.
  • Never heat a bearing with a torch. Uneven heat, no temperature control, and a ruined bearing.
Pulling and pressing
  • A puller under load is a spring. Stand to the side, never in line with the jaws or the screw. Safety glasses at minimum; face shield on large pulls.
  • Hydraulic pullers and pushers: the hose and the fitting are hydraulic injection hazards. Same rules as the hydraulics module.
  • Never hammer a bearing on or off. It brinells the bearing, and a hammer on a hardened race can shatter it. Fragments travel.
  • Press: the bearing is supported on the race being pressed, square, with a sleeve. A cocked bearing under a press lets go sideways.
  • Shrink fits with dry ice or liquid nitrogen: cold burns and oxygen displacement in a closed area.
Grease guns
  • A hand-operated grease gun produces pressures well above what is needed to inject grease through skin. Never hold a fitting or a hose end against a hand. Never test a gun against a finger.
  • A grease injection wound looks like nothing and needs emergency treatment. Tell the triage staff it is a high pressure injection.
  • Pneumatic and battery grease guns are worse. Coupler on the fitting, hands clear, and the gun pointed at the bearing.
Lockout and rotating shafts
  • Checking bearing play by hand means hands on a shaft. Locked out, verified, and the driven side blocked.
  • Listening with a screwdriver on a running housing: hands clear of the coupling, the fan, and the guard openings. The screwdriver goes in; fingers do not.
  • Inspecting a bearing through a housing cover on a stopped machine: is it really stopped, and can it start. Lock it.
Field tip: the bearing goes on onceA heated bearing that stalls halfway down a shaft is a bearing that comes off with a puller and goes in the bin. Dry run the sequence cold: shaft clean and chamfered, bearing oriented, gloves on, where the shoulder is. Then heat it and do it once.
