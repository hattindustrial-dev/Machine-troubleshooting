Motors: Mechanical Side
Module 10
Scope: what this module is and is not
An electric motor is a mechanical machine with an electrical heart. The bearings, the shaft, the fan, the frame, the mounting, the alignment, the coupling, and the load are mechanical, and they cause the majority of motor problems. Windings, connections, insulation, voltage, and drives are electrical. In Ontario, work on the electrical side is the licensed electrician's scope. This module covers everything a millwright owns, and how to hand off cleanly when the problem is not mechanical. Done well, the millwright's checks are what make the electrician's visit a ten minute confirmation instead of a two hour hunt.

The three-phase induction motor in one paragraph
Three-phase power in the stator windings creates a magnetic field that rotates at synchronous speed: 3600, 1800, 1200, or 900 rpm at 60 Hz for a 2, 4, 6, or 8 pole motor. The rotor (a squirrel cage of bars in a laminated core) is dragged along behind that field, a little slower. The difference is slip, and it is what produces torque: more load, more slip, more current. A 4-pole motor nameplated at 1750 rpm is running 50 rpm behind its 1800 rpm field at full load, and closer to 1795 unloaded. That is why measuring shaft speed tells you something about load, and why a motor that has slowed noticeably is a motor that is being overloaded or single phased.
  • Current rises with load, roughly linearly near full load. Below about half load, current flattens out and does not fall much further, because the motor draws magnetising current regardless of load. An unloaded motor draws 25 to 40 percent of full load amps.
  • Starting current is 6 to 8 times full load amps on an across-the-line start. That is normal and is why overloads have a time delay.
  • A VFD changes the frequency to change the synchronous speed. At reduced speed the shaft fan moves less air, which matters for cooling.
  • Single phase motors, DC motors, and servo motors have their own behaviour and their own components (capacitors, brushes, encoders). This module is mostly about the three-phase induction motor because that is most of what is on a plant floor.
Field tip: the instant-off testA motor vibrating or humming: watch or feel it as the power is cut. If the vibration or hum vanishes the instant the power goes off, it was electrical (magnetic forces, unbalanced phases, rotor bar problems). If it decays gradually as the motor coasts down, it is mechanical (unbalance, misalignment, bearings). One test, one second, and you know whose problem it is.

Motor bearings: the mechanical failure
Bearing failure is the most common motor failure by a wide margin, and almost all of it is preventable: over-greasing, under-greasing, belt over-tension, misalignment, contamination, and on VFD-driven motors, electrical fluting. The bearing failure module covers diagnosis in full. This tab is what is specific to motors.
  • Drive end (DE) bearing takes the coupling or belt load. Non-drive end (NDE) takes the fan and locates the rotor axially on most designs. A belt-driven motor fails the DE bearing; a misaligned coupled motor fails the DE bearing; a VFD-driven motor with fluting often fails the NDE first because it is the smaller bearing.
  • Deep groove ball bearings with C3 (extra) internal clearance are standard, because the rotor heats and expands. A standard clearance bearing in a motor runs tight and hot.
  • Small motors have sealed bearings: no grease fittings, no maintenance, replace on failure. Larger motors have regreasable bearings with a fill fitting and, on the good ones, a relief plug or drain.
  • Grease type must match. Mixing incompatible thickeners (lithium complex and polyurea, for instance) turns the grease to soup or to soap. The nameplate or manual specifies it. Polyurea is common in motors.
Over-greasing a motor pushes grease into the windings
The bearing cavity has a fixed volume. Grease pumped in past that volume goes somewhere. With the relief plug in, it goes past the inner bearing cap and into the motor, onto the windings, where it insulates them from cooling air and eventually causes a winding failure that gets blamed on the electrical side. Remove the relief plug before greasing. Grease the specified quantity, slowly, with the motor running if the manual says so. Leave the relief plug out for a run period so the excess purges. Then replace it.
  • Quantity is in the manual, or by the formula G = 0.005 × D × B (grams, bearing OD and width in mm). A 6309 bearing takes roughly 12 to 15 grams, which is a few strokes of a hand gun, not a full tube.
  • Interval depends on speed, size, and temperature. A 2-pole motor needs greasing several times more often than a 6-pole of the same frame.
  • Ultrasonic monitoring while greasing shows the friction drop as the grease reaches the bearing and the rise when there is too much. It is the right way to grease critical motors.
  • A motor that has just been greased and runs hot at the bearing has been over-greased. It is churning. Remove the relief plug and let it purge.
Bearing currents and fluting on VFD motors
A variable frequency drive switches the output voltage thousands of times a second. That switching induces a voltage on the rotor shaft, and the shaft discharges it through the path of least resistance: the bearings. Each discharge is a tiny arc between the ball and the race that melts a pit. Over months the pits line up into a washboard pattern (fluting) across the race, the bearing gets noisy, and it fails. The bearing did nothing wrong.
  • Fluting looks like fine, evenly spaced lines across the race, perpendicular to the direction of rolling. Frosting (a dull grey matte surface) is the early stage. Both are electrical damage, not mechanical.
  • Fixes are on the motor: an insulated bearing (usually NDE), a ceramic hybrid bearing, or a shaft grounding ring that gives the current a path to ground that is not through the bearing. A shaft grounding ring is a retrofit a millwright can do.
  • A motor that is not inverter duty, on a VFD, with a bearing failing every year with a washboard race: this is why.
  • Also check the VFD to motor cable grounding and the drive's carrier frequency setting; both affect bearing currents and both are the electrician's side to adjust.
Shaft loading: what the motor bearings are rated for
  • Radial load rating on the DE bearing sets how much belt tension or overhung sheave load the motor can carry. It is in the motor catalogue. A large sheave on a long shaft extension on a small frame exceeds it.
  • Sheave as close to the motor bearing as the guard allows. Every extra centimetre of overhang multiplies the bending load.
  • Axial load: most motors have a small axial rating, because a deep groove ball bearing is not a thrust bearing. A vertical motor, or a coupling that transmits thrust from the driven machine, needs a motor designed for it.
  • Coupling misalignment loads the DE bearing every revolution. Alignment tolerance for a motor is the same as for any coupled machine; the motor bearings are usually smaller than the driven machine's, so they fail first.
Field tip: listen at the NDEThe NDE bearing is behind the fan, small, and the first to flute on a VFD motor. Screwdriver on the NDE bearing housing (through the fan shroud slots, with the motor running and hands clear of the fan) picks up the gravelly hiss of fluting before it is audible without help. Compare it to the DE. Compare it to an identical motor beside it.
Mounting: soft foot, frame distortion, and the base
A motor frame is a cast or fabricated shell with the stator pressed inside it and the bearing housings in its end bells. The frame's job is to hold the rotor exactly centred in the stator bore, with an air gap of a fraction of a millimetre all round. Bolt the frame down onto a base that is not flat, and the frame twists. The air gap goes uneven, the magnetic pull goes uneven, the motor hums and vibrates at twice line frequency, and the bearings carry a load that changes with every revolution. That is soft foot, and it is the mounting fault that gets diagnosed as an electrical fault.
  • Soft foot check before any alignment: loosen one foot bolt at a time with an indicator on the foot. Any lift over 0.05 mm (0.002 in) is soft foot. Shim it. The alignment module has the full procedure.
  • Four kinds: a short foot (parallel gap under one foot), an angled foot (foot not parallel to the base), a springy foot (bent foot, or dirt or a burr under it), and induced soft foot (pipe strain or coupling strain pulling the frame). Each is corrected differently.
  • Base condition: rust, paint, grout cracks, and old shims left in place all produce soft foot. Clean to bare metal under the feet.
  • Adapter plates and slide bases: a motor on a slide base for belt tensioning has four more surfaces that can be uneven. Check the slide base to the foundation, then the motor to the slide base.
  • Frame distortion from over-torqued or unevenly torqued foot bolts. Torque in a pattern, to the specification for the bolt size, not to the limit of the wrench.
Alignment and the coupling
The coupling and alignment module covers the procedure. Motor-specific points:
  • Thermal growth: a motor grows upward as it heats, and a large motor grows enough to matter. The alignment target may include a cold offset so the shafts are aligned hot. It is in the machine documentation or calculated from frame height and temperature rise.
  • Magnetic centre: the rotor of a sleeve bearing motor (large motors) floats axially and finds its own centre when running. The coupling must be set so the rotor runs at magnetic centre, marked on the shaft. Ball bearing motors locate the rotor and do not have this.
  • Rotor end float on sleeve bearing motors is limited by the coupling on a limited end float coupling. Fitting the wrong coupling lets the rotor hit the bearing thrust faces.
  • Coupling hub fit on the motor shaft: motor shafts are made to a standard tolerance and a hub bored oversize walks and frets. Check the fit.
Field tip: hum that changes when you loosen a foot boltA motor humming with a hard, angry, 120 Hz buzz, and the buzz changes when one foot bolt is backed off half a turn: that is soft foot distorting the frame and the air gap. Nothing electrical was ever wrong. The electrician was about to megger it.
Heat: where it comes from and why hand-on-the-frame lies
A motor turns electrical energy into shaft power and heat. The heat is the losses: copper losses in the windings (rising with the square of current), iron losses in the core, friction and windage. A 95 percent efficient 100 HP motor makes about 4 kW of heat at full load, continuously, and the frame and fan have to get rid of it. The winding temperature limit is set by the insulation class, and every 10°C above the class limit roughly halves the insulation life.
Insulation classMax winding temperatureTypical frame surface at full load, 40°C ambient
B130°C60 to 75°C
F155°C70 to 90°C
H180°CUp to 100°C
  • A frame you cannot hold your hand on can be a healthy class F motor. The hand test tells you almost nothing. An infrared reading, compared to the same motor last month or to the identical motor beside it, tells you a lot.
  • Design ambient is 40°C. Above that, the motor is over its thermal rating at nameplate load. A motor in a hot enclosure, near an oven, or in a summer roof space is derated by the ambient.
  • Altitude derates cooling above about 1000 m. Not usually an Ontario problem.
  • Frame temperature that has risen from its usual is the symptom. The absolute number is only meaningful against the insulation class and the ambient.
Cooling paths by enclosure
  • TEFC (totally enclosed fan cooled): a shaft-driven fan at the NDE blows air over the finned frame under a shroud. Nothing enters the motor. The fins, the shroud inlet, and the fan are the cooling system. Blocked fins or a shroud packed with dust or product, and the motor overheats at any load.
  • ODP (open drip proof): air is drawn through the motor, over the windings, and out. Cooler and cheaper, and it eats whatever is in the air. Vent screens blocked with lint or fibre are the usual fault.
  • TENV (totally enclosed non-ventilated): no fan, cooled by the frame surface alone. Small motors and servo motors. Depends on the mounting surface and free air around it.
  • Blower cooled (TEBC): a separate small motor drives the cooling fan, so cooling is independent of shaft speed. Used on VFD motors that run slow. If the blower fails, the main motor overheats at any speed.
  • VFD at low speed: a shaft fan at 20 percent speed moves almost no air. A TEFC motor on a VFD needs a derate below about 50 percent speed at full torque, or a blower, or it cooks.
Sources of excess heat, mechanical side
  • Overload: the driven machine is asking for more than nameplate. Amps tab.
  • Misalignment and belt over-tension: bearing friction, and current rising to overcome it.
  • Bearing failing: the DE or NDE end hot, frame body normal. A localised hot spot is a bearing; a uniformly hot frame is load, cooling, or electrical.
  • Fan damaged, loose on the shaft, or running backward: a reversed motor rotation on a motor with a unidirectional fan halves the airflow.
  • Frequent starts: each start dumps 6 to 8 times FLA into the windings. Too many starts per hour overheats a motor that never runs above half load. The manufacturer publishes the starts per hour limit.
  • Grease in the windings from over-greasing insulates the winding from the cooling air.
Field tip: the fan shroud is a filter nobody changesPull the shroud on a TEFC motor that has been in a dusty area for a few years. The fins are full, the fan is caked, and the motor has been running twenty degrees hotter than it should for as long as anyone remembers. Compressed air (with the motor locked out and eye protection on) and a stiff brush. Add it to the PM.
Current draw is a mechanical symptom
The motor draws the current it needs to turn the load. Read the amps and compare to the nameplate FLA and you know how hard the motor is working. A millwright reading amps off the panel ammeter or a drive display is reading the load, and load is a mechanical question: what is the driven machine asking for, and why. Clamping a meter on the leads is the electrician's side in most Ontario plants; reading a permanently installed meter or a VFD display is not.
Percent load ≈ (measured amps ÷ nameplate FLA) × 100
Valid from about 50% load upward. Below that, amps flatten and the estimate reads high.

Example: 20 HP motor, FLA 24 A at 575 V
Reads 26 A → about 108% load: over nameplate, into the service factor
Reads 12 A → somewhere under 50%: lightly loaded, cannot tell exactly
Reads 8 A → unloaded (magnetising current only)
  • Read all three phases if you can. They should be within a few percent of each other. One phase different is an electrical problem (unbalanced supply, a bad connection, a winding fault): hand it off with the readings.
  • Read under the actual operating condition, and read it again when the fault is happening. Amps at idle tell you nothing about amps at the jam.
  • Trend it. The same motor, the same load, the same reading month after month. A reading that has risen is the load growing or the mechanical losses growing. A reading that has fallen is a belt slipping, a coupling gone, or a pump losing prime.

What lowers amps
  • Belt slipping: the motor is not doing the work. Amps down, driven speed down, belt squeal.
  • Coupling or key sheared: motor at unloaded amps, driven machine stopped.
  • Pump lost prime or running dry: amps drop sharply. A dry-running pump seal is minutes from failure.
  • Fan damper closed, or a blocked duct: a fan draws less air and less power when starved. This one surprises people.
  • Product stopped: an empty conveyor or an empty mixer.
Speed as a symptom
A tachometer or strobe on the motor shaft gives slip, and slip gives load, without touching anything electrical. A 4-pole motor at 1795 rpm is unloaded. At 1750 it is at full load. At 1720 it is overloaded. At 1650 something is badly wrong: overload, low voltage, or single phasing, and that last one will burn the windings in minutes.
Field tip: uncouple and run soloHalf of all motor arguments end with this test. Lock out, uncouple (or remove the belts), and run the motor alone. Amps at the unloaded value, quiet, cool, no vibration: the motor is fine and the problem is in the driven machine or the coupling. Still hot, noisy, high amps, or vibrating: the motor has a problem, and now you can tell the electrician it was tested uncoupled.
The handoff: what to give the electrician and what is theirs
A good handoff is a page of readings, not a shrug
The electrician who arrives to a motor that has been mechanically cleared, with the amps, the speed, the temperatures, and the uncoupled test written down, can go straight to the electrical checks. The one who arrives to "the motor's acting up" starts from zero and spends the first hour doing the mechanical checks you could have done. The handoff package is how a millwright makes the whole maintenance team faster.
The handoff package
  • Nameplate photo.
  • Symptom, when it started, what changed.
  • Amps per phase from the panel or drive, under load and unloaded if possible.
  • Shaft speed if measured.
  • Infrared temperatures: frame body, DE bearing, NDE bearing, and the same points on a comparable motor.
  • Result of the instant-off test: vanished instantly (electrical) or decayed (mechanical).
  • Result of the uncoupled solo run.
  • Bearing condition: sound, play, temperature.
  • Soft foot and alignment status.
  • Cooling: fins, shroud, fan condition.
  • Greasing history: when, how much, relief plug in or out.
  • Whether it is on a VFD, and whether the bearings show fluting.
What is theirs
These are the electrician's checks. Knowing what they are lets you recognise when a fault is heading their way and describe it in their terms.
  • Insulation resistance (megger): winding to ground. Low means moisture, contamination, or insulation breakdown.
  • Winding resistance balance: the three phases should match. An open or shorted winding shows here.
  • Supply voltage and balance: low voltage raises current and heat; a few percent voltage unbalance causes a much larger current unbalance and a lot of heat.
  • Single phasing: one phase lost, motor runs on two, hums, slows, and overheats fast. A blown fuse, a bad contactor tip, a loose connection.
  • Connections: loose or corroded terminals in the peckerhead, the starter, or the disconnect. Heat at a connection is found with infrared on an open panel, which is their job with the right PPE.
  • Overload and protection settings: an overload set wrong trips a healthy motor, or fails to protect an overloaded one.
  • VFD parameters: motor data entered, carrier frequency, volts-per-hertz curve, acceleration ramps, and the drive's own fault log.
  • Rotor bar problems: broken rotor bars show as a rhythmic swing in current at slip frequency and a growl under load. Electrical signature analysis finds them.
  • Capacitors on single phase motors, brushes on DC motors, encoders on servos.
Scope in Ontario
Electrical work, including connecting and disconnecting motor leads, live testing, and work inside panels and junction boxes, is the scope of a licensed electrician under the Ontario Electrical Safety Code and the trade regulations. A millwright's scope is the mechanical machine. Applying your own lock to a disconnect as part of a group lockout is yours. Opening the peckerhead is not. Where the line sits exactly in a given plant is a matter of the plant's policy and the collective agreement, and the safe assumption is that anything behind a cover with a voltage warning is theirs.
Motor fault diagnosis: mechanical side first

1. Look and listen, running. Infrared at DE bearing, NDE bearing, and frame body. Screwdriver at both bearing housings. Hand on the frame for rhythm. Fan shroud and fins. Read the amps and speed if a meter or drive display is there.

2. Instant-off test. Vibration or hum that vanishes the instant power is cut is electrical. Decays with speed is mechanical.

3. Lock out, turn by hand. The motor shaft and the driven shaft separately if possible. Free, smooth, no rough spots, no drag. Rotor does not rub. Fan spins free and is tight on the shaft.

4. Soft foot and alignment. Loosen one bolt at a time with an indicator on the foot. Then the alignment readings.

5. Uncoupled solo run. Motor alone, no load. If it is clean, the problem is downstream. If it is not, you have isolated it to the motor and you have the readings to hand over.

After these five, a fault that is still unexplained is on the electrical side, and the handoff package tells the electrician where to start.

Motor safety: electrical hazards a millwright works beside
The motor is connected to enough energy to kill you, and it stays connected until the disconnect is open and locked
A stopped motor is a live motor until the disconnect is opened, locked, tagged, and verified. A motor on a VFD can have the drive stopped and the motor leads still live. A motor with a capacitor bank, a soft starter, or a drive can hold a charge after the disconnect is opened. Your lock on the disconnect is your protection. Verify the isolation by attempting a start, and never assume a stop button, a drive keypad, or a PLC is an isolation. CSA Z462 is the Canadian standard for electrical safety in the workplace and defines the arc flash and shock boundaries; the plant's electrical safety program applies them.
Lockout for motor mechanical work
  • Open the local disconnect (or the breaker if there is no local disconnect), apply your lock and tag, and try to start the motor from every start point: local, remote, HMI.
  • Motors on VFDs: the disconnect must be upstream of the drive, or the drive's own input disconnect. A stopped drive is not an isolation. Some drives have DC bus capacitors that hold a lethal charge for minutes after isolation; the drive manual states the discharge time and the electrician verifies it before anyone opens the drive.
  • Group lockout: your lock on the group lockbox, the electrician's isolation of the source. You do not remove your lock until your work is complete and you are clear.
  • Mechanical energy: the driven machine can turn the motor. Block the driven side. A fan in a duct windmills from draft; a conveyor with a load on an incline runs back; a pump with a head of liquid behind a leaking check valve spins backward.
  • Never work on a motor with a cover off the terminal box unless it is isolated, verified, and you are qualified to be there.
Rotating parts and the fan
  • The shaft key on an uncoupled motor is a rotating blade. Remove the key or tape it before running a motor solo. A loose key thrown from a shaft at 3600 rpm is a projectile.
  • The fan shroud is a guard. A motor run with the shroud off has an exposed fan at 1800 or 3600 rpm.
  • Screwdriver stethoscope on the NDE housing through the shroud slots: the screwdriver goes in, fingers stay out. Keep the tool short and the grip firm.
  • Loose clothing, lanyards, and gloves near an uncoupled running shaft: the shaft grabs and wraps.
Heat and burns
  • A class F motor frame at full load can be 90°C. That is a burn. Infrared before hand.
  • A motor that has been single phasing or stalled can have a frame well over 100°C and windings that are smoking. Isolate and let it cool before anything else.
  • Grease from a failing bearing can reach smoking temperature. Smell of hot grease from a motor is a stop.
Handling and lifting
  • Lift by the lifting eye on the frame. The eye is rated for the motor alone; a motor with a gearbox, a pump, or a base attached has a different weight and a different centre of gravity.
  • Never lift by the shaft, the fan shroud, or the terminal box.
  • Rigging is RigIt's territory. Weight from the nameplate or catalogue, sling angle, and rated hardware.
  • Motors in wash-down and food areas are heavy for their size (stainless, cast). Do not judge by the frame number.
