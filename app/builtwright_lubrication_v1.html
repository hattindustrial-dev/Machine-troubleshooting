All lubricants are built from a base oil plus an additive package. The base oil determines the fundamental properties: viscosity, viscosity index, pour point, oxidation stability, and volatility. The additives modify and extend those properties for specific applications.

GI
Group I base oils are solvent-refined mineral oils: the traditional refining process. They have the most impurities, the lowest oxidation stability, and the shortest service life. Largely replaced by Group II in most industrial lubricants.
GII
Group II base oils are hydrotreated mineral oils with lower impurity levels, better oxidation stability, and longer service life than Group I. The standard for most industrial and automotive lubricants today.
GIII
Group III base oils are severely hydrotreated mineral oils with very high purity and viscosity index. Often marketed as synthetic or semi-synthetic. Performance approaches Group IV in many applications.
GIV
Group IV: PAO (Polyalphaolefin) is a true synthetic base oil built from polymerised alpha-olefin molecules. Extremely uniform molecular structure gives excellent viscosity index, low pour point, and outstanding oxidation stability. PAO is the most common true synthetic in industrial use.
GV
Group V: Esters, PAG, and others cover all synthetic base oils that do not fit Groups I through IV. Esters (diesters, polyol esters) have excellent lubricity and solvency. PAG (polyalkylene glycol) is used in natural gas compressors, food grade, and some gear applications. Not compatible with mineral oils without checking: PAG in particular requires a dedicated system.

ISO Viscosity Grades: Industrial lubricants use the ISO VG system. ISO VG 32, 46, 68, 100, 150, 220, 320 are the common grades for hydraulic and gear oils respectively. The number is the nominal kinematic viscosity in centistokes (cSt) at 40 degrees Celsius. A higher number means a thicker oil at that temperature. The correct grade for a given application is determined by the equipment manufacturer's specification and the operating temperature range. Never substitute a higher or lower ISO grade without checking the manufacturer specification.

Viscosity Index (VI): describes how much the oil's viscosity changes with temperature. A high VI oil changes viscosity less across a temperature range than a low VI oil. Synthetic oils generally have higher VI than mineral oils, which is why they perform better across wider temperature ranges. Multigrade motor oils use VI improver additives to achieve a high VI from a lower base VI oil.

Additive packages include anti-wear (AW) agents, extreme pressure (EP) agents, oxidation inhibitors, rust and corrosion inhibitors, foam inhibitors, demulsifiers, and viscosity index improvers. EP additives (typically sulfur-phosphorus compounds) activate under high contact stress to form a protective surface film. Do not assume all gear oils with the same viscosity grade are interchangeable: the additive packages differ and some are incompatible with certain metals or seals.

Centralized lubrication systems: types and failure modes
Centralized lubrication systems deliver the correct amount of lubricant to multiple points automatically, on a timed or metered basis. When functioning correctly they eliminate human error from the lubrication task. When they fail, they do so silently: the equipment continues to run while lubrication points starve, and the damage accumulates invisibly until a bearing or component fails. Understanding how each system type works is essential to recognising when it is not working.

Contamination: the primary cause of lubrication failure
Studies consistently show that 70 to 80 percent of premature component failures in lubricated machinery are contamination-related. The lubricant does not wear out: it gets contaminated. Particle contamination, water ingress, oxidation products, and wrong lubricant introduction each have distinct signatures and distinct consequences. Identifying the contamination type is the first step toward both remediation and prevention.
Particle contamination

Particle contamination is the most damaging and most common form of lubricant contamination. Particles smaller than the lubricant film thickness (often just a few microns) cause the most damage because they pass through filters, enter the contact zone, and embed in or scratch the bearing surfaces without being visible to the naked eye.

Sources: ingression through breathers and seals, particles generated by internal wear (self-contamination), dirty maintenance practices (contaminated oil cans, funnels, work surfaces), and new oil that has not been filtered to the required cleanliness level before use.

  • New oil from the drum is not clean. Most new oil from a drum is ISO cleanliness code 18/16/13 or dirtier. Many precision systems require 16/14/11 or better. Filter all new oil before introduction.
  • Breathers on reservoirs and gearboxes are the primary ingression point for airborne particles. Desiccant breathers with particulate filtration are one of the highest-return contamination control investments.
  • ISO 4406 cleanliness code reports particle counts at 4, 6, and 14 micron sizes. A code of 17/15/12 means fewer than 130,000 particles per mL greater than 4 microns, fewer than 32,000 greater than 6 microns, and fewer than 4,000 greater than 14 microns.
  • The Millipore patch test: pass a measured volume of oil through a membrane filter and examine the patch under magnification. The colour, density, and character of the deposit identifies contamination type and approximate level.
  • Each ISO cleanliness code step represents a doubling of particle count. Moving from code 18 to code 16 is a 4x reduction in particles: a significant improvement in bearing life.
Water contamination

Water in oil is destructive in multiple ways simultaneously. It reduces lubricant film strength, promotes corrosion of metal surfaces, accelerates oxidation of the base oil and additive depletion, supports microbial growth in some fluids, and at low temperatures can freeze in clearances and cause catastrophic seizure.

Visual identification: dissolved water (below saturation) is invisible. Emulsified water gives the oil a milky or cloudy appearance. Free water settles to the bottom of a reservoir or sample bottle as a distinct water layer. The crackle test, placing a drop of oil on a hot plate, will produce crackling and spattering if water is present above approximately 500 ppm.

  • Sources: condensation from thermal cycling (common in outdoor equipment and equipment with frequent start-stop cycles), process water ingress through failed seals, wash-down water through breathers, and steam condensate in steam-adjacent equipment.
  • Acceptable water content varies by application. For most rolling element bearing systems, less than 100 ppm (0.01 percent) is the target. For hydraulic systems, less than 200 to 500 ppm depending on the system pressure and sensitivity.
  • Milky oil in a gearbox is a red flag. The gear mesh and bearings are operating without adequate film strength. Change the oil, identify and fix the water ingress source, and inspect internal components before returning to service.
  • Desiccant breathers, improved shaft seals, and keeping reservoirs full (reducing air space for condensation) are the primary water ingress controls.
Oxidation and thermal degradation

Oil oxidises when exposed to heat and oxygen over time. Oxidation produces acidic compounds and varnish (a hard, amber to brown lacquer-like deposit) that coats surfaces, restricts flow through orifices and valves, and accelerates further degradation. Thermal degradation at very high local temperatures (hot spots from inadequate lubrication, electrical discharge) produces carbon deposits and sludge rather than varnish.

Visual identification: oxidised oil darkens progressively from amber to dark brown to black. Varnish deposits appear as hard, shiny, amber or brown coatings on metal surfaces inside reservoirs, on valve spools, and on bearing retainers. Sludge is soft and dark, settles in low-flow areas of the system.

  • The rate of oxidation roughly doubles for every 10 degrees Celsius increase in operating temperature. An oil system running 10 degrees hotter than designed will consume its oxidation inhibitor package in roughly half the expected service interval.
  • Acid number (AN or TAN: total acid number) measured in oil analysis is the primary indicator of oxidation. Rising TAN indicates oxidation inhibitor depletion and acid accumulation. A TAN that has doubled from the new oil value is typically a change indicator.
  • Varnish deposits in hydraulic systems are particularly serious because they stick proportional valves and servo valves in intermediate positions, causing erratic or failed machine control.
  • Varnish potential index (MPC: membrane patch colorimetry) is an oil analysis test specifically designed to detect varnish precursors before deposits form.
Cross-contamination: wrong lubricant

Cross-contamination occurs when the wrong lubricant is introduced to a system, either by topping up with an incompatible product or by using contaminated dispensing equipment. The consequences range from additive package incompatibility (causing precipitation of additive compounds) to material incompatibility (certain synthetic base oils attacking elastomeric seals) to complete loss of the lubricant's required properties.

Common scenarios: hydraulic fluid added to a gear oil system (additive packages differ significantly), PAG-based compressor oil mixed with a PAO or mineral oil system (PAG and mineral oil are not miscible), food grade lubricant contaminated with non-food-grade product in a food processing facility.

  • Colour-coded dispensing equipment (dedicated containers, funnels, and hose ends for each lubricant) is the most effective prevention against cross-contamination in the field.
  • Lubricant identification tags or barcodes on equipment lube points eliminate reliance on memory when there are multiple lubricant types in a facility.
  • When cross-contamination is suspected, take a sample for analysis before draining. Knowing what got mixed in determines whether the system needs a flush or a simple drain and refill.
  • Never mix greases of different thickener types without confirming compatibility. Lithium complex and polyurea greases are incompatible: the mixture softens, loses structure, and runs out of the bearing housing.
  • When changing lubricant type or brand on a recirculating system, consult the new lubricant supplier about flush procedures. Some transitions require a flush oil run to remove residual additive incompatibility before filling with the new product.
Microbial contamination

Microbial contamination (bacteria and fungi) occurs in water-containing fluid systems: primarily water-based metalworking fluids, water-glycol hydraulic fluids, and oil-water emulsion systems. Microbes consume additive components, produce acidic metabolic byproducts, and generate visible slime or biofilm. Microbial contamination in a pure mineral oil system is rare but possible if water is present.

Visual and sensory identification: rotten or sulfurous smell, dark sludge deposits in the reservoir, rapid pH drop in monitored systems, and visible slime in low-flow areas. The Monday morning smell (foul odour when opening a machine that sat over a weekend) is a classic indicator of anaerobic bacterial activity.

  • Biocide addition at the correct concentration and on the correct interval controls microbial populations in metalworking fluids. Underdosing allows resistant strains to establish. Overdosing may cause dermatitis in operators.
  • Maintaining the correct fluid concentration (monitored by refractometer for emulsion-type coolants) is the first line of defence. Dilute coolant is more susceptible to microbial growth.
  • Removing tramp oil (from machine slideways and hydraulics leaking into the coolant sump) removes the nutrient source for bacteria and significantly improves sump life.
  • Microbial contamination in a mineral oil system (rare) is evidenced by slime deposits, rapid oil darkening, and foul odour. Full drain, clean, and refill is required. Identify and eliminate the water source.
Field tip: the drum is not cleanNew oil arrives with a particle count that would fail most hydraulic and gearbox targets. It has been through a refinery, a tank farm, a truck, a drum, and a warehouse. Filter it into the machine through a transfer cart with a filter, and keep the drum sealed with a desiccant breather. Clean oil is made at the fill point.
Oil analysis: reading the evidence in the fluid
Oil analysis is a predictive maintenance tool that examines a sample of in-service lubricant to assess the condition of the oil, the condition of the machine, and the cleanliness of the system. A single analysis gives a snapshot. A series of samples trended over time gives a far more powerful picture, revealing gradual changes in wear rate or oil condition that would not be apparent from any single data point. The value of oil analysis is in the trend, not the number.
How to sample correctly: the most overlooked step
01
Sample from the same location every time
Results are only comparable if samples come from the same point in the system. The ideal sampling point is a dedicated sampling valve installed in a location with turbulent flow, typically in the return line before the filter. Never sample from the drain plug (gets sludge and settled contamination) or the top of the reservoir (gets surface scum and condensation). Consistent sampling location is the single most important factor in getting comparable trending data.
02
Sample while the machine is running or immediately after shutdown
Wear particles and contaminants are suspended in the oil during operation. After shutdown they begin to settle. Sampling a machine that has been off for hours gives artificially clean particle count results. Sample during operation or within 15 minutes of shutdown for accurate results.
03
Use a clean, dedicated sampling kit
Contaminating the sample during collection invalidates the result. Use a new sampling tube and bottle for each sample. Do not use a funnel or container that has been used for anything else. Flush the sampling port with a small amount of oil before taking the actual sample to purge any stagnant fluid from the sample line.
04
Record machine hours, lubricant hours, and any recent events
The lab report is only useful if it is contextualised. Record: total machine hours, hours since last oil change, hours since last top-up and the quantity added, any abnormal operating conditions or incidents since the last sample, and any maintenance performed. This information allows the analyst to distinguish between a real trend and an anomaly caused by a recent event.
Key oil analysis parameters: what each tells you
Viscosity at 40°C and 100°C
Oil Condition
The most fundamental oil property. A viscosity significantly above the new oil value indicates oxidation, contamination with a higher viscosity product, or evaporation of lighter fractions. A viscosity significantly below the new oil value indicates dilution with a lighter fluid (fuel dilution in engine oils, solvent contamination, or addition of wrong product). A change of more than 10 percent from the new oil reference value warrants investigation.
Total Acid Number (TAN)
Oil Condition
Measures the total acid content of the oil in mg KOH per gram of oil. New oil has a baseline TAN established from the product data sheet. Rising TAN indicates oxidation and additive depletion. A TAN that has doubled from the new oil baseline is typically a change indicator. In systems handling acidic process fluids, TAN can rise from external contamination rather than oil degradation: distinguish between the two when interpreting a high TAN result.
Total Base Number (TBN)
Oil Condition
Relevant primarily for engine oils and other applications where acidic combustion products are neutralised by alkaline additives. TBN represents the oil's remaining capacity to neutralise acids. When TBN falls below approximately 50 percent of the new oil value (the exact limit depends on the application), the oil's acid-neutralising reserve is depleted and should be changed. TBN is not typically reported for hydraulic or gear oils.
Particle Count: ISO 4406
Contamination
Reports the number of particles per millilitre at three size thresholds (greater than 4, 6, and 14 microns). Expressed as an ISO cleanliness code (e.g. 17/15/12). The target cleanliness code is application-specific: high-pressure servo hydraulics may require 15/13/10 or better, while a general gear oil system may accept 18/16/13. Each step in the ISO code represents a doubling of particle count: the scale is logarithmic, not linear.
Water content: ppm or percent
Contamination
Measured by Karl Fischer titration (quantitative) or crackle test (qualitative field check). Acceptable limits vary by application. For most rolling element bearing systems the target is below 100 ppm. Hydraulic systems typically accept 200 to 500 ppm depending on pressure class. Gear oils are generally more tolerant but milky appearance (above approximately 1000 ppm emulsified water) always warrants action. Trending is essential: a slowly rising water content indicates a developing ingression path.
Elemental spectroscopy: wear metals
Wear
ICP (inductively coupled plasma) spectroscopy identifies and quantifies metals dissolved or suspended in the oil. Iron, chromium, nickel, and copper indicate wear of steel, chrome-plated, or bronze components respectively. Aluminium may indicate wear of aluminium pump housings or bearing cages. Silicon indicates dirt ingestion (from soil or airborne particles). Lead and tin may indicate bearing alloy wear. Each element is tracked as a trend: a sudden increase in a wear metal indicates accelerated wear of the corresponding component.
Ferrography: wear particle morphology
Wear
Ferrography captures and examines ferrous wear particles on a glass slide under a microscope. The shape, size, and surface characteristics of the particles indicate the type and severity of wear. Normal rubbing wear produces small, smooth particles. Cutting wear (abrasion) produces long, thin curled particles. Fatigue wear produces flat, angular platelets with surface pits. Severe sliding wear produces large, irregular particles with rough surfaces. Ferrography answers the question that spectroscopy cannot: what type of wear is occurring, not just how much metal is in the oil.
Additive elements: phosphorus, zinc, calcium
Fluid ID
Additive elements from the lubricant's additive package (zinc and phosphorus from ZDDP anti-wear additives, calcium from detergent/dispersant additives) deplete as the additives are consumed in service. A decrease in additive element concentrations relative to the new oil reference indicates additive depletion and reduced lubricant protection. This is particularly relevant for hydraulic fluids and gear oils with extended drain intervals.
Application and intervals: how much, how often
More lubricant is not better: this applies everywhere
This is the single most common lubrication mistake across all equipment types. Overlubrication causes more failures than underlubrication in most industrial environments. Excess grease in a bearing housing causes churning, heat, accelerated oxidation, and eventual seal blowout. Excess oil in a gearbox causes foaming, elevated temperature, and seal leakage. The correct amount is a calculated quantity based on bearing size, housing volume, and application, not a judgement call made with a grease gun. When in doubt, calculate. When you cannot calculate, use less than you think you need and monitor the result.
Grease quantity calculation
The correct grease relubrication quantity for a rolling element bearing can be estimated from the following formula, which is used by most major bearing manufacturers:
G = 0.005 × D × B
G = grease quantity in grams
D = bearing outside diameter in mm
B = bearing width (total width) in mm
This formula gives the quantity for a relubrication interval. For initial fill of a new or cleaned bearing housing, fill to 30 to 50 percent of the free space in the housing: never pack completely full. For a relief-plug equipped housing, apply the calculated quantity with the relief plug open and run the machine for 30 minutes to allow excess to purge before reinstalling the plug.
Grease interval factors
A calendar-based interval applied uniformly to all bearings is an oversimplification that results in both over and undergreasing on different pieces of equipment. The correct interval depends on bearing size, operating speed, operating temperature, environmental conditions, and the grease type. The SKF and NSK grease interval calculators (available on their websites) take all these factors into account and should be used as the starting point for any PM interval setting.
  • Operating temperature is the single biggest interval modifier. A bearing running 15 degrees Celsius above the reference temperature needs roughly half the interval.
  • Vertical shaft bearings need more frequent greasing than horizontal shaft bearings because gravity works against grease retention in the contact zone.
  • Contaminated environments (dust, moisture, chemicals) require shorter intervals or sealed bearings with no regreasing required.
  • Ultrasonic lubrication monitoring removes the guesswork entirely: grease until the ultrasonic signal drops to baseline, then stop. This technique prevents both under and overgreasing regardless of what the PM schedule says.
  • Document every lubrication event: date, grease type, quantity, and any observations. This record is essential for root cause analysis when a bearing fails.
Oil change intervals
Oil change intervals should be based on oil analysis results, not on a fixed calendar schedule. A schedule-only approach changes oil that is still serviceable and misses oil that has degraded ahead of schedule due to abnormal conditions. Oil analysis allows extending intervals on lightly loaded, clean systems and shortening intervals when contamination or degradation is detected.
  • The manufacturer's recommended interval is a starting point based on average conditions. Adjust based on actual oil analysis data from your specific equipment and environment.
  • Initial oil change after commissioning a new gearbox or machine is important. Run-in wear produces a higher initial wear metal load that should be removed early. Follow the manufacturer's break-in oil change recommendation.
  • Top-up oil must be the same product as the in-service oil. Adding a different brand or grade dilutes the additive package and may introduce incompatible components.
  • When topping up a system, record the quantity added. Excessive top-up consumption indicates a leak that must be found and fixed, not just compensated for.
Grease compatibility: do not mix without checking
Greases are made from a base oil, a thickener, and an additive package. When two incompatible greases are mixed, the thickener structures can interact in ways that soften the combined mixture, causing it to lose structure and run out of the bearing, or in some cases harden excessively. Neither outcome is acceptable.
  • Lithium and lithium complex greases are compatible with each other but not with polyurea, calcium sulfonate, or aluminium complex greases.
  • Polyurea greases are widely used in electric motor bearings (pre-filled, sealed for life). Adding any other grease type to a polyurea-filled bearing is incompatible in most cases.
  • When changing grease type on a bearing that will be regreased, flush the old grease out completely before introducing the new product. Packing in new grease on top of old incompatible grease produces the worst of both worlds.
  • When in doubt, contact both grease manufacturers and request a compatibility statement. Do not rely on general compatibility charts alone: batch-to-batch variations can affect compatibility.
Field tip: weigh your grease gun oncePump ten strokes onto a kitchen scale. Divide by ten. Now you know what a stroke of your gun delivers, and the grease quantity formula turns into a stroke count. Write the number on the gun with a paint pen. Every gun is different, and a gun that delivers 1.5 g per stroke at room temperature delivers less in January.

Lubrication safety: injection, fire, and the lube room
Lubricants are the safest looking materials in the plant, and they injure people every year.
Grease guns inject. Hot oil burns. Oil mist and oil-soaked rags ignite. Spilled oil puts people on the floor. Centralized systems run at hundreds of bar. Treat lubrication as fluid handling, with the same rules as any other pressurised, flammable, slippery fluid.
Injection injury from grease guns and centralized systems
  • A hand grease gun develops enough pressure to push grease through skin. Never test a coupler against a hand, never hold a hose end, and never clear a blocked fitting by pointing the gun at anything but the fitting.
  • Centralized grease systems (progressive, dual-line) run at 100 to 400 bar. A line that has been cut, a fitting that has cracked, or a blocked point that is being cleared can spray at injection pressure. Depressurise the line before opening it.
  • An injection wound looks like a puncture. It is a surgical emergency. Tell the triage staff it is a high pressure injection and what the material was.
  • Pneumatic and battery grease guns: coupler on the fitting before the trigger, hands behind the gun.
Hot oil and fire
  • Gearbox and hydraulic oil at 60 to 90°C burns on contact. Drain into a container that will not deform, and let it cool before handling.
  • Oil-soaked rags self-heat. Metal bin with a lid, emptied daily. A pile of oily rags in a corner is a fire waiting for a warm afternoon.
  • Oil mist lubrication systems and oil sprays near hot surfaces are ignition hazards. Mist collects on surfaces and in ducts.
  • Compressed air on an oil-soaked surface aerosolises it. Aerosolised oil is flammable and breathable.
  • Aerosol chain lubes and penetrating oils are flammable propellants. Not near hot work.
Handling and skin
  • Prolonged skin contact with mineral oils causes dermatitis and worse. Nitrile gloves for oil handling, and wash before eating. Used oil is dirtier than new oil in ways that matter to skin.
  • Some EP additives, some synthetics, and all used oils carry hazard warnings on the SDS. Read it for the products you handle every day, not just the exotic ones.
  • Oil on the floor is the plant's most common slip and fall. Clean it now, not at the end of the job. Absorbent on hand at every lube point.
Lube room and storage
  • Drums stored on containment, indoors, labelled, with the lids on and the bungs tight. A drum open to the plant air is a drum full of dirt and water in a month.
  • Each product with its own dedicated pump, container, and grease gun, colour coded. Cross-contamination is a reliability problem and an incompatibility reaction is a chemical problem.
  • Reservoirs and tanks large enough to enter are confined spaces. Permit, atmosphere test, attendant.
  • Used oil is a regulated waste. Segregated, labelled, and collected by a licensed hauler. Never down a drain.
  • Spill kit sized for the largest container in the room, and everyone knows where it is.
Field tip: the coupler goes on firstA grease gun trigger pulled while the coupler is being lined up on a fitting sends a jet of grease at whatever is in front of it, at a pressure that goes through a glove. Coupler on, seated, hand clear, then the trigger. Every time, on every fitting, until it is a reflex.
