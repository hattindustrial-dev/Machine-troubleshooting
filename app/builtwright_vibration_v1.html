Module 11
What this module is for
Three modules in this series (bearings, alignment, gearboxes) say things like "parallel misalignment shows as 2x" and "bearing defects are non-synchronous" without explaining what that means or how you would see it. This module fills that gap. It is not a vibration analyst course. It is what a millwright needs to take a good reading, read a basic spectrum, know what the analyst is talking about, and recognise when a machine is telling you something. A Category I or II vibration analyst certification is the next step for anyone who wants to own this. This module is the on-ramp.
What vibration is, and the three ways to measure it
A rotating machine vibrates because something in it produces a force that repeats every revolution, or every few revolutions, or every time a ball passes a defect. The vibration you measure at the bearing housing is the sum of all those forces, each at its own frequency. Separating them by frequency is the whole trick. The amplitude at each frequency tells you how big each force is. Change over time tells you which one is growing.
MeasureUnitsBest forWhy
Displacementmicrometres, mils (peak to peak)Low speed machines under 600 rpm, shaft movement on sleeve bearings, proximity probesHow far it moves. Large at low frequency, tiny at high frequency.
Velocitymm/s, in/s (RMS or peak)General machinery 600 to 60,000 cpm. The standard for severity.How fast it moves. Roughly flat across the useful range, so one number means the same thing at any speed. ISO severity charts use velocity.
Accelerationg, m/s²High frequency: bearings, gear mesh, above about 1 kHzHow hard it is being shaken. Emphasises high frequency, where bearing and gear faults live.
  • Velocity in mm/s RMS is the number to trend and compare. When someone says a machine is running at 4 mm/s, that is what they mean.
  • Frequency is in Hz (cycles per second), cpm (cycles per minute, which is Hz × 60), or orders (multiples of running speed). Orders are the most useful for diagnosis because 1x, 2x, and 3x mean the same thing on any machine regardless of its speed.
  • An overall reading is one number for the whole vibration at that point. A spectrum splits it by frequency. The overall tells you something changed. The spectrum tells you what.
  • Phase is the timing of the vibration relative to a reference mark on the shaft. It separates faults that look alike in the spectrum (unbalance versus a bent shaft, for example). It needs a tachometer reference and is an analyst tool.
Severity: what the numbers mean
ISO 20816 (formerly ISO 10816) gives velocity zones by machine size and foundation type. These are guidance for overall velocity in mm/s RMS at the bearing housing, and the machine's own baseline matters more than the chart. A machine that has run at 5 mm/s for ten years is different from one that went from 1 to 5 last month.

  • Rigid base: the machine's lowest natural frequency is above its running speed (bolted to a heavy concrete foundation). Flexible base: below (on a steel frame, on isolators). Most floor-mounted pumps and fans on concrete are rigid; most rooftop units and structural-steel-mounted machines are flexible.
  • Small machines under 15 kW have their own group with slightly lower limits. Reciprocating machines and machines with integral gearboxes have separate standards.
  • A change of 2.5 times the baseline, or a move from one zone to the next, is the usual trigger for investigation regardless of the absolute number.
Field tip: the pen meter is enough to startA handheld vibration pen that gives overall velocity, used at the same three points on the same machines every month and written in the same logbook, catches the majority of developing failures. The spectrum analyser tells you what the fault is. The pen tells you there is one. Start with the pen, and get the analyser when the logbook is full.
Taking a reading that means something
Where
At each bearing, as close to the bearing as you can get, on solid metal, in three directions: horizontal (H), vertical (V), and axial (A). A pump and motor set has four bearings, so twelve points. The same twelve points every time, forever. Mark them with paint or a stamped dot.
  • Horizontal is usually the highest on a horizontal machine because the mounting is least stiff sideways. Unbalance shows most in H.
  • Vertical reflects the foundation and base. Looseness and soft foot show in V.
  • Axial is along the shaft. Misalignment, bent shafts, and thrust problems show in A. A high axial reading is always interesting.
  • Never measure on a fan shroud, a guard, a thin cover, or a bracket. They resonate and lie. Bearing housing, end bell, or a solid part of the frame directly over the bearing.
  • Motor NDE behind the fan shroud is the awkward one. Many shrouds have a slot for it; some plants weld a stud.

  • Bearing defect frequencies and their harmonics run from a few hundred Hz to several kHz. A magnet mount is the minimum for bearing diagnosis. A handheld probe cannot see them.
  • Same sensor, same mount, same point, same load, same speed. Any of those changing changes the reading and the trend is broken.
  • Machine at normal operating temperature and normal load. A cold, unloaded machine reads differently from the same machine in production.
  • Write down the speed, the load, and anything unusual with every reading. A spectrum without the running speed cannot be read in orders.
Analyser setup basics
  • Fmax (maximum frequency) set to cover what you want to see: for general faults, 10 to 20 times running speed; for bearings and gear mesh, 3 to 5 times the highest bearing or mesh frequency, often 5 to 10 kHz.
  • Resolution (lines): more lines separate close frequencies (sidebands, pole pass, close harmonics). 1600 to 3200 lines is typical for a route; more for diagnosis.
  • Averages: 4 to 8 averages smooth random noise. Overlap saves time.
  • Windowing: Hanning for routine spectra. Leave it there unless the analyst says otherwise.
  • Take a time waveform too. Impacts (a bearing defect, a broken tooth, looseness) are obvious in the waveform and can be lost in the spectrum.
Field tip: the paint dotA spot of paint, a stamped centre punch mark, or a dab of nail polish at every measurement point. It costs nothing and it is the difference between a trend and a pile of numbers. Two people taking readings a centimetre apart on the same housing get different values. The dot fixes that.
Reading a spectrum: frequency is the fingerprint
A spectrum is amplitude (vertical) against frequency (horizontal). Each peak is a force at that frequency. The first thing to do with any spectrum is find running speed (1x) and label the horizontal axis in orders. Then every peak falls into one of a few families, and each family has a short list of causes.

Field tip: find 1x firstNothing in a spectrum makes sense until you know where running speed is. On a motor it is near 1800 or 3600 cpm (30 or 60 Hz) minus the slip. On a belt-driven fan it is the fan speed, which is the motor speed times the sheave ratio. On a gearbox output it is the input speed divided by the ratio. Mark 1x, then 2x, then 3x. Everything else is read relative to those.
Rolling element bearing frequencies and the four stages of failure
A rolling element bearing with a defect on a race produces an impact every time a ball or roller hits the defect. The rate of those impacts depends on the bearing geometry and the shaft speed, and it is never a whole multiple of running speed. That is what non-synchronous means, and it is why a peak at, say, 3.58x is a bearing and not a machine fault. Each bearing has four characteristic frequencies. Bearing manufacturers publish them, and analyser software has them in a database by bearing number.
n = number of rolling elements, d = ball diameter, D = pitch diameter, φ = contact angle, f = shaft speed

BPFO (outer race) = (n/2) × f × (1 - (d/D) cos φ)
BPFI (inner race) = (n/2) × f × (1 + (d/D) cos φ)
BSF (ball spin) = (D/2d) × f × (1 - ((d/D) cos φ)²)
FTF (cage) = (f/2) × (1 - (d/D) cos φ)

Rule of thumb without the geometry:
BPFO ≈ 0.4 × n × f    BPFI ≈ 0.6 × n × f    FTF ≈ 0.4 × f
Example: 8-ball bearing on a 1780 rpm shaft (29.7 Hz): BPFO ≈ 95 Hz (3.2x), BPFI ≈ 142 Hz (4.8x)
  • BPFO and BPFI always add up to n × f. If you see two non-synchronous peaks and they sum to the ball count times running speed, they are the same bearing.
  • Inner race defects rotate with the shaft, so the impact moves in and out of the load zone once per revolution: BPFI comes with 1x sidebands. Outer race defects are stationary in the load zone: BPFO is usually clean, with harmonics.
  • Ball defects produce BSF, often at 2 × BSF because the defect hits both races, with FTF sidebands.
  • Cage frequency (FTF) alone, sub-synchronous, is a cage problem or a badly worn bearing.
  • The bearing number is on the nameplate or the drawing. With the number, the software gives exact frequencies. Without it, the rules of thumb get you close enough to know it is a bearing.
The four stages
StageWhat is happeningWhat you see and hearRemaining life, roughly
1Microscopic subsurface cracksUltrasonic only (20 to 60 kHz). Enveloping, spike energy, HFD, or an ultrasonic gun pick it up. Nothing in the normal spectrum. Nothing audible.10 to 20% of life left
2Small pits forming, ringing the bearing components at their natural frequenciesA hump in the 500 Hz to 2 kHz range in acceleration. Enveloping shows the defect frequency clearly. Still quiet.5 to 10%
3Defects large enough to produce clear impactsDiscrete BPFO or BPFI peaks in the velocity spectrum, with harmonics, then sidebands. Audible with a screwdriver. Slight temperature rise.1 to 5%
4Defects spreading, geometry breaking down, clearances openingDiscrete peaks blur into a broadband hump (the haystack). 1x rises as looseness sets in. Hot. Noisy without help. Metal in the grease.Hours to days
  • Stage 3 is when most plants catch it and it is fine: weeks of notice, plan the change. Stage 4 is the emergency.
  • Enveloping (demodulation) filters out the low frequency machine vibration and looks only at the repetitive impacts. It finds stage 1 and 2 faults that the velocity spectrum cannot see. Every modern analyser has it; use it on every bearing point.
  • A bearing that goes straight from clean to stage 4 in a week was probably damaged on installation, or is being destroyed by something (fluting, contamination, a load it was not designed for).
Field tip: envelope every bearing point, even when the overall is fineThe overall velocity does not move until stage 3. The envelope spectrum moves at stage 1. On a route, collecting an envelope reading at every bearing takes an extra ten seconds per point and adds months to the warning. Set the analyser to take both automatically.
Trending: the change is the information
A single reading tells you how a machine is running today against a chart made for machines in general. A trend tells you how this machine is running against how it ran last month and last year, and that is far more sensitive. A pump that has always run at 3.5 mm/s is fine at 3.5. The same pump at 3.5 after ten years at 1.5 is in trouble. The baseline is the first reading after commissioning or after a rebuild, taken when the machine is known good, and everything after is compared to it.
  • Baseline: three readings in the first few weeks after a rebuild, averaged. Not the first hour after start, when the bearings are still seating.
  • Alarm levels: an alert at roughly 2 times baseline (or the next ISO zone), a danger at 2.5 to 3 times baseline (or two zones up). Set them per machine, not plant-wide.
  • Band alarms: separate alarms on the 1x band, the 2x band, the bearing band, and the high frequency band. A rise in one band tells you what is changing before the overall moves.
  • Rate of change matters more than level. A reading that doubled in a month is urgent. A reading that doubled in five years is wear.
  • Collection interval: monthly for general machines, weekly for critical or known-problem machines, continuous (online) for the ones that stop the plant.
  • Keep the readings with the machine record, with the work orders. A trend that jumps and the work order that explains it (a bearing change, a realignment) is the history that makes the next call easy.
A route in practice
  • List every machine with its bearings, points, speeds, bearing numbers, and gear tooth counts. This is the setup work that makes every later reading useful.
  • Same order, same points, same conditions, same person if possible.
  • Note anything unusual on the machine as you go: a new noise, a leak, a hot spot, a recent work order.
  • Review the trends after the route, not the raw readings. A reading is a number. A trend is a conversation with the machine.
  • Anything that has crossed an alarm gets a diagnostic reading: higher resolution, more averages, envelope, time waveform, and a look at the machine.
When to hand off to an analyst
A Category II or III analyst reads the fine structure: phase, sideband families, resonance, rotor dynamics, electrical signatures. The millwright with a route and a trend brings them a machine that has changed, the readings that show it, and a first read on the family (synchronous, bearing, gear, electrical). That handoff is the same as the motor handoff: it turns a hunt into a confirmation.
  • Hand off: readings that have crossed alarm with no obvious mechanical cause after the basic checks (soft foot, alignment, tension, looseness, bearing condition by hand).
  • Hand off: anything with sidebands you cannot explain, anything electrical, anything on a sleeve bearing machine, anything that looks like resonance.
  • Do not hand off: a 1x rise on a fan with a visibly dirty wheel. Clean it, re-read it.
Field tip: the logbook beats the software until the software has the logbook in itPlants buy analysers and never build the machine database, so every reading is an orphan. Before the analyser, build the list: machine, bearings, points, speeds, tooth counts, sheave ratios. It is a week of work and it is the whole program.
Resonance: when the structure amplifies the fault
Every structure has natural frequencies at which it likes to vibrate: a bell rings at its natural frequency when struck. If a machine produces a force at or near a natural frequency of its base, its frame, a pipe, or its own rotor, that force is amplified, sometimes by ten times or more. A small unbalance becomes a machine that shakes the building. The fault is small; the structure is the problem. Resonance is the reason two identical pumps on two identical bases can behave completely differently.
  • Signs: high vibration at one frequency that does not respond to the fix you expect (a balanced fan still shaking at 1x), vibration that changes a lot with a small speed change on a VFD, vibration much higher in one direction than the others at the same point, and a base or pipe that you can feel vibrating more than the machine.
  • Bump test: with the machine stopped and locked out, strike the structure with a soft-faced hammer while the analyser records. The peak in the resulting spectrum is the natural frequency. If it is within about 15 percent of running speed, 2x, blade pass, or any other forcing frequency, resonance is amplifying that force.
  • VFD sweep: run the machine slowly through its speed range and watch the overall. A sharp peak at one speed is a resonance. Program the drive to skip that speed band.
  • Critical speed is a rotor's own resonance: the shaft bows at that speed. Machines are designed to run well below the first critical (stiff shaft) or above it (flexible shaft, passing through it quickly on startup). A machine that shakes at one speed on run-up and is fine at running speed is passing through a critical.
Fixing resonance
Three levers: stiffness, mass, and damping. Stiffening a structure raises its natural frequency; adding mass lowers it; damping reduces the amplification without moving the frequency much. The goal is to move the natural frequency away from the forcing frequency by 15 to 20 percent, or to add enough damping that the amplification is tolerable.
  • Stiffen: gussets, a thicker base, grouting a hollow base, bracing a pipe, shortening an unsupported span. The most common and most effective fix on a fabricated steel base.
  • Mass: rare as a fix, because it lowers the frequency and usually you want it higher. Sometimes a mass on a pipe or a bracket moves a local resonance out of the way.
  • Damping: isolators, viscous dampers, constrained-layer damping on panels. Reduces the peak. Isolators under a machine also decouple it from the structure, which is a different but related fix.
  • Change the forcing frequency: skip the speed on a VFD, change the sheave ratio, change the blade count. Sometimes the cheapest option.
  • Reduce the force: balance better, align better. Resonance amplifies whatever is there; less input, less output.
Field tip: feel the base, not just the machinePut your hand (or the sensor) on the base, the sole plate, the anchor bolts, and the nearest pipe. If any of them vibrate more than the bearing housing, the structure is part of the problem. A base that hums louder than the pump on it is a resonant base, and the twentieth balance job will not fix it.
From reading to cause

1. Is it real, and is it new? Check the reading against the baseline and the trend. Check that the sensor was on the right point, on clean metal, with the machine at normal load and speed. A bad reading is more common than a bad machine.

2. Find 1x. Running speed from a tachometer, a strobe, or the nameplate and the sheave ratio. Label the spectrum in orders.

3. Which family? Synchronous (whole multiples of 1x), sub-synchronous, non-synchronous, harmonic series, sidebands, or broadband. The family narrows the cause to a handful.

4. Which direction? Radial (H or V) versus axial. High axial says misalignment, bent shaft, or thrust. Vertical higher than horizontal says looseness or a base problem. Horizontal dominant says unbalance.

5. Which bearing? Compare the same frequency at every point. The point with the highest amplitude is nearest the source. A fault across the coupling (high on both machines at the coupling end) is alignment.

6. Go and look. The spectrum says unbalance; the fan wheel is caked with product. The spectrum says looseness; a foot bolt is loose. The spectrum says bearing; the bearing is hot and growling. The machine confirms the spectrum.

7. Instant-off test for anything that could be electrical. Vanishes instantly: electrical. Decays: mechanical.

Safety: measuring on running machines
Vibration readings are taken on running machines. That is the hazard.
Every other module locks the machine out before hands go near it. This one puts a sensor on a bearing housing while the shaft is turning, next to the coupling, the fan, the belts, and the guard openings. The sensor goes where the guard allows it. If the reading point is inside a guard, the guard gets a stud or an access hole fitted during a lockout, not a hand reached through it during a route.
Hands, cables, and magnets
  • Never reach through, around, or under a guard to place a sensor. Fit a permanent stud or an access port during a lockout instead.
  • The sensor cable is a loose line next to rotating parts. Keep it short, keep it in hand, never let it drape over a shaft or a coupling. A cable wrapped on a shaft takes the analyser and your hand with it.
  • Magnet mounts are strong. Keep them away from the shaft, the coupling, and any rotating steel. A magnet that snaps onto a rotating part is a projectile.
  • Loose clothing, lanyards, gloves, and hair near a running shaft: the usual rule, with a reminder that vibration routes have you leaning close to machines for a living.
  • A handheld probe pressed on a housing is a stick next to a shaft. Firm grip, short reach, and know where the rotating parts are before you press.
Bump tests and stud installation
  • A bump test is done on a stopped, locked out machine. The hammer, the sensor placement, and the person all go near the machine only after lockout is verified.
  • Drilling and tapping a stud into a bearing housing is a lockout job with chip control: no swarf into the bearing or the guard.
  • Adhesive pads: the surface prep (solvent, abrasive) is done locked out.
Heat, noise, and height
  • Bearing housings on a hot machine are hot. Gloves for the sensor, not for the shaft.
  • Hearing protection on the route. A vibration route is hours next to the loudest machines in the plant.
  • Reading points on fans, rooftop units, and overhead drives are at height. Fall protection and a plan for both hands being on the instrument.
  • A stage 4 bearing can let go. If a machine is in zone D and getting worse, the reading is taken from as far away as the cable allows, and the machine is scheduled down.
