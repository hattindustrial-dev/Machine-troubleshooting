How a pneumatic system works
A pneumatic system converts compressed air into mechanical work. Air is drawn in from the atmosphere, compressed to the working pressure (typically 6 to 10 bar in industrial systems), stored in a receiver tank to buffer demand, conditioned to remove moisture and contamination, and then directed through control valves to actuators that perform the physical work. The system chain is always the same: generate, store, condition, control, and act. A fault anywhere in that chain affects everything downstream.
  • Pneumatic systems are open-loop with respect to the air supply: spent air exhausts to atmosphere rather than returning to a reservoir as in hydraulics.
  • Air is compressible. This gives pneumatic actuators a natural cushioning quality but also means speed control requires flow restrictors, not just pressure.
  • Moisture is the enemy of pneumatic components. Water vapour compressed into the system condenses in valves, cylinders, and airlines, causing corrosion, icing in cold conditions, and contamination of lubricated components.
  • The system pressure is set at the regulator for each circuit branch. The compressor runs at a higher pressure than any branch requires, and the regulators step it down to the correct working pressure for each application.
  • When troubleshooting a pneumatic system, always start at the air supply: is there pressure? Is the pressure correct? Is the air dry and clean? These three questions eliminate the majority of pneumatic faults before touching any valve or actuator.
Field tip: read the gauge at the machine, not at the compressorThe compressor room gauge says 7 bar. The machine that is misbehaving is 200 metres of undersized pipe and three loaded filters away, and its regulator is seeing 4.5 bar when every cylinder on the line strokes at once. A gauge tee at the machine inlet, read while the machine is running, is the only pressure that matters to that machine.

Proportional directional control valves use an electrically controlled actuator (voice coil, stepper motor, or proportional solenoid) to position the valve spool at any point between fully closed and fully open. Flow rate and direction are proportional to the electrical command signal. Used for variable speed control of actuators or precise positioning without a full servo system.

Pressure relief valves set the maximum system pressure by opening to exhaust when pressure exceeds the set point. They are safety devices and must be sized for the full compressor output flow. A relief valve lifting frequently means system demand exceeds supply, or a downstream regulator has failed open.

Pressure reducing valves (regulators) maintain a set downstream pressure regardless of upstream fluctuations. A regulator that cannot maintain set pressure under flow may be undersized, have a contaminated seat, or have a seized adjustment spring.

Flow control valves restrict flow in one direction while allowing free flow in the other through an integral check valve. Meter-out (restrict exhaust from actuator) is preferred for most cylinder applications: it provides consistent speed regardless of load variations and prevents cylinder runaway under a pulling load.

Quick exhaust valves are placed at the cylinder port to exhaust air directly to atmosphere, dramatically increasing speed in one direction by reducing exhaust restriction.

Shuttle valves (OR valves) have two inlets and one outlet. The higher-pressure inlet signal closes the lower-pressure inlet and passes to the outlet. Used when an actuator or signal must respond to either of two control signals.

Two-pressure valves (AND valves) only pass a signal when both inlets are simultaneously pressurised. Used as a safety interlock: requiring two simultaneous operator inputs before a cycle can initiate.

Valve manifolds mount multiple directional control valves on a common body that shares a single air supply gallery and one or two exhaust galleries. This simplifies machine plumbing dramatically: one supply connection and one exhaust silencer serve all valves on the manifold. The pilot supply on many manifolds is a separate 24VDC-controlled air rail, which can fail independently of the main working pressure. A manifold system where no valve is shifting but working pressure is correct is often a failed pilot supply. Manifold exhaust silencers are a common source of hidden restriction: one partially blocked silencer adds back-pressure to the shared exhaust gallery and slows every valve on the manifold in the exhaust direction. Always include the exhaust silencer condition in manifold troubleshooting.

Field tip: the manual override is a diagnostic toolMost solenoid valves have a manual override button or screw. Press it with the power to the coil confirmed off. If the valve shifts and the actuator moves, the pneumatics are fine and the problem is the coil, the wiring, or the PLC output. If nothing moves, it is the valve, the air, or the actuator. One press splits the problem in half. Return the override to its normal position before walking away.

Cylinder force calculation
Extend and retract force
The force a cylinder produces depends on bore diameter, rod diameter (retract stroke), and working pressure:
Extend force (N) = (π / 4) × D² × P
Retract force (N) = (π / 4) × (D² - d²) × P

D = bore diameter in metres
d = rod diameter in metres
P = working pressure in Pascals (1 bar = 100,000 Pa)

Example: 63mm bore, 20mm rod, 6 bar
Extend: (π/4) × 0.063² × 600,000 ≈ 1,868 N ≈ 190 kg force
Account for friction losses of 10 to 20 percent. Apply a minimum safety factor of 1.5 for load-bearing applications.

Air quality: the foundation of reliable pneumatics
Compressed air quality is the foundation of reliable pneumatic system performance. Atmospheric air contains water vapour, oil aerosols, dust, and microorganisms. When compressed to 7 bar, all contaminants are concentrated by a factor of seven. Without conditioning, those contaminants flow into every valve, actuator, and instrument in the system.
Water in compressed air: the primary enemy
Water vapour in compressed air condenses as the air cools after compression. This liquid water causes corrosion of valve bores and cylinder bores, washes lubrication from components, causes solenoid valve spools to stick, produces icing in cold applications, and in food or pharmaceutical applications contaminates the product. Drying compressed air is not optional on any system where reliability matters.
  • Refrigerant dryers cool air to approximately 3°C pressure dewpoint: adequate for most industrial applications.
  • Desiccant dryers achieve pressure dewpoints of minus 20 to minus 70°C: used for instrument air, outdoor cold climate applications, and moisture-sensitive processes.
  • Drain all receiver tanks, aftercoolers, and drip legs regularly. Automatic drain valves on a timed cycle are more reliable than manual draining.
  • A system producing water at the point of use has either an inadequate dryer, a failed dryer, or automatic drains that are not functioning.
FRL units: filter, regulator, lubricator
An FRL unit is the standard point-of-use air conditioning assembly. The filter removes particles and bulk water, the regulator sets working pressure, and the lubricator introduces oil mist for components that require it. The three functions must happen in that order: filter before regulate before lubricate.
  • Drain the filter bowl regularly. Replace the filter element when pressure drop exceeds approximately 0.5 bar.
  • Set the regulator under load conditions, not at no-flow. Pressure drops under flow: set to achieve correct pressure at the actuator.
  • Set the lubricator to 1 to 2 drops of oil per minute into the air stream at operating flow. Excess lubrication accumulates in exhaust silencers and work areas.
  • Not all pneumatic components require lubrication. Modern pre-lubricated valves and cylinders can be harmed by oil mist washing out factory lubrication. Confirm before installing a lubricator.
  • ISO 8573 defines compressed air quality classes. Know the class required for your application before specifying filtration equipment.
Tubing materials and sizing
The tubing between a directional control valve and the cylinder is part of the pneumatic circuit, and its bore diameter and material both affect system performance. Undersized tubing between a valve and actuator is one of the most common causes of slow cylinders that is misdiagnosed as a valve or pressure problem: the valve is fine, the pressure is fine, but the tube cannot pass enough air fast enough to fill the cylinder at the required speed.
  • Polyurethane (PU) tubing is the most common in industrial pneumatics: flexible, good pressure rating at room temperature, easy to cut and connect. Pressure rating drops significantly above 50°C. Not suitable for prolonged UV exposure.
  • Nylon (PA12) tubing has higher temperature and UV resistance than PU. Preferred for outdoor installations, environments with chemical splash, and anywhere PU would degrade. Slightly stiffer than PU.
  • PTFE tubing is used for high-temperature applications, food-grade requirements, and chemical resistance. Less flexible, harder to work with, and more expensive. Required in applications where PU or nylon would swell or degrade.
  • Tube bore sizing: the tube bore between valve and cylinder must be large enough to allow the cylinder to fill and exhaust at the required cycle rate. As a general rule, use the same bore size as the valve port the tube connects to, and minimise the tube length. Every metre of undersized tube adds measurable restriction.
  • Push-in fitting collet wear: the collet teeth that grip the tube in a push-in fitting wear over time, particularly if the tube has been inserted and removed repeatedly. A tube that pulls out without releasing the collet has failed gripping teeth. Replace the fitting.
System pressure and flow sizing
An undersized compressed air system causes intermittent faults that appear random. When multiple actuators demand air simultaneously, pressure drops across the distribution system. Valves do not shift reliably, cylinders do not reach full force, and pressure-confirmation interlocks may not trigger. These faults are very difficult to diagnose without pressure monitoring at the point of use under operating conditions.
  • Measure pressure at the actuator, not at the compressor or main header. A 2 bar drop from compressor to point of use is not unusual on an undersized distribution system.
  • Leakage in a typical industrial compressed air system is 20 to 30 percent of compressor output. An ultrasonic leak detector survey is one of the most cost-effective maintenance activities in any compressed air system.
  • The receiver tank buffers peak demand. An undersized receiver causes excessive compressor cycling, increasing wear and reducing compressor life.
  • Distribution pipe bore is critical. A main that is correct for current load may become undersized when additional machines are added. Calculate pressure drop for each addition.
Temperature effects on pneumatic systems
Temperature affects compressed air systems in ways that are not always obvious. Cold and hot extremes both create problems, and a system that runs reliably in one season may develop intermittent faults when the temperature changes, particularly in facilities without climate control or in outdoor installations.
  • Cold environments: water in the system that would remain liquid at normal temperatures will freeze at valve orifices, cylinder ports, and exhaust silencers as expanding air drops below 0°C. A desiccant dryer with a low enough dewpoint is the solution: the pressure dewpoint must be below the coldest temperature in the system, including outdoor piping runs. A refrigerant dryer is not adequate for below-freezing outdoor applications.
  • Cold seals: at very low temperatures (below minus 10 to minus 20°C depending on material), standard nitrile and polyurethane seals stiffen and lose their sealing ability. Low-temperature seal compounds (silicone, low-temp nitrile) are required for cold stores, outdoor equipment in northern climates, and similar applications.
  • FRL lubricator viscosity: the oil in a lubricator does not flow at very low temperatures. The system may start unlubricated until the oil warms up. Use a low-viscosity pneumatic lubricating oil in cold environments, or switch to components pre-lubricated for life and eliminate the lubricator.
  • Hot environments: elevated ambient temperature increases the water vapour content of the air entering the compressor. A refrigerant dryer designed for average conditions may be overloaded during summer or in hot production areas, producing wetter air than specified. Monitor dewpoint readings in summer if the system shows seasonal moisture problems.
  • PU tubing in heat: polyurethane tubing that is continuously exposed to temperatures above 50 to 60°C softens, deforms at push-in fittings, and eventually fails. Replace with PA12 nylon tubing in hot areas near ovens, presses, or heat sources.
  • Solenoid coil ratings: solenoid coils have a thermal class rating (commonly Class F or Class H). In hot environments with high duty cycles, verify the coil ambient temperature rating is not being exceeded. An undersized coil in a hot enclosure will have a shortened service life.
Field tip: a drain that never drains is a drain that has failedCrack the manual drain on every filter bowl and the receiver once a week and watch what comes out. If a bowl that should be collecting water is dry and the next one downstream is full, the first one is bypassing. If the receiver blows a litre of water, the auto drain is not working and every dryer and filter downstream has been fed water for a month.
Pneumatic system fault diagnosis

The most effective approach to pneumatic troubleshooting is bracketing: systematically moving a pressure gauge through the system to identify where the pressure is correct and where it is not. Every pneumatic fault can be located to a specific segment of the system chain this way, regardless of the symptom.

Step 1: Confirm the symptom under operating conditions. Watch the machine run through a cycle if it is safe to do so. Identify exactly what happens and what should happen. A fault that cannot be reproduced cannot be diagnosed reliably.

Step 2: Start at the compressor. Is the receiver at the correct pressure? Is the compressor running? If the receiver is low, the problem is on the supply side. If the receiver is correct, move downstream.

Step 3: Check pressure at the FRL regulator outlet under operating conditions (not at rest). If the regulated pressure is correct at rest but drops under load, the supply is undersized for the demand. If the regulated pressure is below set point even at rest, the regulator, filter, or upstream supply is the problem.

Step 4: Check pressure at the valve inlet port. If correct here but the actuator is not moving or is weak, the fault is in the valve or downstream of it. If pressure is low at the valve inlet, there is a restriction or leak between the FRL and the valve.

Step 5: Check pressure at the actuator port while the valve is commanded to shift. If pressure is building at the actuator port but the actuator is not moving, the fault is in the actuator itself (seized piston, mechanical blockage). If pressure is not building, the valve is not shifting or is blocked.

Step 6: Rule out sensor and feedback faults before condemning pneumatic components. The most common cause of "cylinder moved but machine didn't advance in the cycle" is a position sensor that did not confirm the cylinder reached its end position, not a pneumatic fault at all. Before replacing valves or cylinders, confirm that all position sensors are wired, powered, correctly positioned, and producing the expected output signal when the actuator is at the detection point. See the sensor faults entry in the symptom tree below.

Methodical pressure checks take five minutes. Random component swapping can take five hours. Always bracket first.

Field tip: screwdriver stethoscope. In a loud production environment where you cannot hear a leak, a shifting valve, or a humming solenoid over the ambient noise, press the tip of a screwdriver firmly against the component body and hold the handle end lightly against the bone directly behind your ear. Bone conduction carries the sound through the tool and through your skull, cutting through background noise without putting your ear near a pressurised exhaust or a moving part. Works for tracing air leaks, confirming whether a valve spool is actually shifting when commanded, and listening for internal mechanical faults in any rotating or reciprocating component. It costs nothing and you already have one in your pocket.

Field tip: the screwdriver stethoscope finds the leak the ear cannotA long screwdriver with the handle to your ear and the tip on a fitting, a cylinder end cap, or a valve body turns a hiss you cannot locate into one you can. A leaking piston seal hisses at the exhaust port of the valve; a leaking rod seal hisses at the rod end. Ten seconds at each fitting on a manifold beats a bottle of soap water, and it works while the machine is running from outside the guard.

Pneumatic system safety: stored energy and hazard control
Compressed air is stored energy: treat it that way
Compressed air at 6 to 10 bar contains significant stored energy. A sudden release, from a failed fitting, a cracked hose, or breaking a pressurised connection, can cause serious injury. Air injected into the skin at even 1 bar can cause an air embolism. Never assume a pneumatic system is de-energised because the machine is stopped or the power is off. Compressed air remains in lines, cylinders, receivers, and manifolds until it is actively released.
Stored energy: verify before you touch
Pressure bleed-off before breaking any connection
Stopping a machine does not depressurise a pneumatic system. Actuators, manifolds, supply lines, and receiver tanks all retain pressure after the compressor stops and the machine is powered down. Before cracking any fitting, disconnecting any hose, or removing any valve:
  • Isolate the air supply at the machine isolation valve (lockout/tagout the energy isolation point).
  • Exhaust the system using the dump valve or manual bleed valve if fitted. If not, carefully loosen a fitting downstream of the isolation point at a safe angle away from your body and face: never point the fitting toward yourself or another person.
  • Confirm pressure is at zero on a gauge before fully removing any component. A gauge reading zero on one branch does not mean the entire system is de-energised: check at the point you are working.
  • Receiver tanks retain pressure even after all other system pressure is released. The receiver isolation valve must be closed and the receiver pressure verified at zero before working on the receiver or any piping directly connected to it.
  • Spring-loaded accumulators and cylinders under load can move suddenly when air pressure is released. Support loads mechanically before de-energising the circuit holding them.
Tube whip and fitting ejection
A pressurised hose or tube that is disconnected or fails can whip violently and strike personnel or equipment with significant force. Fittings ejected under pressure become projectiles. Both are serious injury hazards.
  • Never pull a push-in fitting release collar while the circuit is pressurised. Always de-pressurise and verify zero pressure before disconnecting push-in tubing.
  • Inspect hoses and tubing for damage: kinks, abrasion, UV degradation, and heat damage all weaken the tube wall and reduce burst pressure. A hose that shows cracking or stiffness in a flexible section should be replaced before it fails in service.
  • Do not exceed the rated working pressure of hose, tubing, or fittings. The working pressure rating is not the burst pressure: do not use burst pressure as the working limit.
  • Hose restraints (safety cables or whip checks) must be fitted on any high-pressure hose where a coupling failure would allow the hose to whip. This is particularly important on hoses connected to the compressor output or receiver.
Pressure ratings and temperature limits
Every component in a pneumatic system, hose, tubing, fittings, valves, cylinders, receivers, carries a stamped or labeled maximum working pressure and temperature rating. These ratings must not be exceeded. Operating above rated pressure risks sudden failure of the component with no further warning.
  • Match the pressure rating of the weakest component to the maximum system pressure, not just the normal operating pressure. A pressure spike from a water hammer event or compressor fault can exceed normal operating pressure significantly.
  • Polyurethane (PU) tubing is the most common push-in tubing in industrial pneumatics: good flexibility and good pressure rating at room temperature. Its pressure rating drops significantly above 50°C. In hot environments, nylon (PA) or PTFE tubing is more appropriate.
  • Nylon (PA12) tubing has higher temperature resistance than PU and is preferred for outdoor applications and environments with UV exposure or chemical splash.
  • PTFE tubing is used for high-temperature applications, chemical resistance, and food-grade requirements. It is less flexible and harder to work with than PU or nylon.
  • Never substitute a component with a lower-rated replacement simply because it fits. Fitting the correct rated component is not optional.
Hazards specific to compressed air
Compressed air hazards are frequently underestimated compared to hydraulic or electrical hazards, but they are serious. The specific hazards of compressed air differ from other energy sources and are worth knowing explicitly.
  • Air injection injury: compressed air injected into the skin, even through a small pinhole leak held against the skin, can cause an air embolism that travels to the brain or heart. This can be fatal. Never use compressed air to clean skin or clothing, and never hold a pressurised line or fitting against the body.
  • Noise: pneumatic exhaust noise is impulsive and high-frequency. Exhaust silencers are not just for courtesy: they are noise control for hearing protection. Repeated exposure to unsilenced exhaust can cause permanent hearing damage.
  • Flying debris: air blowoff can entrain and accelerate particles at high velocity. Eye protection is mandatory when using compressed air for blowoff or cleaning operations.
  • Cold burns: expanding air from a high-pressure exhaust or a compressed CO₂ or nitrogen source can be cold enough to cause frost burns on contact with skin.
  • Oxygen displacement: compressed nitrogen or CO₂ used in pneumatic-style circuits will displace oxygen in confined spaces. Never work in a confined space where a nitrogen or CO₂ line is present without confirming oxygen levels and using confined space entry procedures.
Lockout / tagout for pneumatic systems
Pneumatic systems require energy isolation under lockout/tagout (LOTO) before any maintenance work on the equipment they power. The steps are the same as for any energy source, but pneumatics has specific requirements for confirming de-energisation that differ from electrical systems.
  • The isolation point for a pneumatic system is the machine air inlet valve, typically a lockable ball valve between the main supply header and the machine FRL unit. This valve must be locked in the closed position.
  • After closing the isolation valve, actuate all directional control valves (manually or through the control system if safe to do so) to exhaust trapped air from actuator ports and valve manifolds. Many machines have a dump valve or exhaust valve specifically for this purpose.
  • Verify zero pressure at the working point with a calibrated gauge before beginning work. A gauge that cannot be zeroed independently should not be trusted.
  • Residual energy in spring-loaded actuators is mechanical, not pneumatic: the spring remains loaded even after air pressure is released. Treat a spring-return cylinder as a loaded spring after de-pressurisation and support the load mechanically before proceeding.
  • In Ontario, LOTO requirements for pneumatic systems fall under Ontario Regulation 851, Section 75 (Industrial Establishments) for the machinery guarding and control of hazardous energy. Procedure must be written, posted, and followed.
