Ratio, speed, and the numbers you actually use
Speed ratio: N1 × D1 = N2 × D2 (rpm × pitch diameter, either side)
Driven rpm = Driver rpm × (Driver PD ÷ Driven PD)
Belt speed (m/min) = π × PD (m) × rpm
Chain: ratio = driven teeth ÷ driver teeth

Example: 1750 rpm motor, 150 mm driver sheave, 400 mm driven sheave
Driven rpm = 1750 × (150 ÷ 400) = 656 rpm
Belt speed = π × 0.15 × 1750 = 825 m/min (about 2700 ft/min)
Use pitch diameter, not outside diameter, for belt calculations. The pitch line is where the belt's tension cords run, a little below the top of the belt. Sheave catalogues list pitch diameter. For chain, sprocket pitch diameter is a function of tooth count and chain pitch and is in the sprocket catalogue.
  • Classical V-belts are comfortable up to roughly 1500 m/min (5000 ft/min). Narrow section belts run faster. Above the belt's rated speed, centrifugal force lifts the belt out of the groove and grip falls off.
  • Minimum sheave diameter matters as much as ratio. A belt bent around a sheave smaller than its rated minimum fatigues the cords and cracks the underside early. The belt catalogue lists the minimum for each section.
  • Chain speed limits depend on pitch: small pitch chain runs fast, large pitch chain runs slow. Above the limit, the chain hits the sprocket teeth hard enough to hammer them.
  • Service factor: the catalogue horsepower rating is multiplied down for shock loads, long hours, and hard starts. A drive sized without a service factor is a drive that eats belts.
Field tip: the drive tells you how it has been treatedRubber dust under the guard is a belt that slipped or ran misaligned. A polished, shiny groove is a worn sheave. A chain with a black paste on it is a chain that has been run dry and ground its own wear metal into old lube. A tensioner backed all the way out is a belt or chain that has stretched to its limit. Five seconds looking before touching anything.

Classical and narrow sections are not interchangeable and neither are the sheaves. A B belt in an A groove rides too high and rolls over. An A belt in a B groove bottoms out and slips. Metric SP sections are close to but not identical to their inch cousins; a mixed drive wears fast.
  • Multiple belt drives use a matched set: belts from the same production batch with matched length codes. Never replace one belt of a set. The new one carries all the load until it stretches to match the old ones, then they all share a shortened life.
  • Belt storage: cool, dry, off the floor, not hanging on a nail. Ozone from motors and welding degrades rubber. A belt that has been on the shelf five years is not a new belt.
  • Belt ride: the top of a V-belt should sit at or slightly above the sheave outside diameter. A belt riding low in the groove means the groove is worn or the belt is the wrong section.
Field tip: sheave groove gaugeA worn groove has a dish in the side walls where the belt has polished it. The belt rides lower, contacts less wall, and slips. Belt manufacturers give away plastic groove gauges. Hold the gauge in the groove against a light: if you can see daylight between gauge and wall, the sheave is worn and new belts will not fix it. A worn sheave kills a new belt set in weeks.

Chain lubrication: it is not optional
A roller chain is a series of small journal bearings (pin in bushing) that see the full drive load every time they articulate around a sprocket. Chain wear is pin and bushing wear, and that wear is elongation. An unlubricated chain wears at many times the rate of a lubricated one. The lube has to reach the pin, which means it goes on the inside of the chain at the gap between the side plates, not on the rollers.
MethodHowUsed for
Manual or dripBrush or drip feed to the lower strand at the sprocket entry, inside edgeSlow drives, low power
Bath (oil disc)Lower strand runs through an oil bath, or a disc throws oilMedium speed, enclosed drives
Oil stream (pump)Pump delivers filtered oil to each strand continuouslyHigh speed, high power, always enclosed
  • Use a light oil that will penetrate to the pin. Grease on the outside of a chain seals dirt in and does nothing for the pin. Some spray chain lubes are designed to penetrate then thicken; they work if applied to the inside edge.
  • Apply to the slack strand where the chain is about to wrap the sprocket, so centrifugal force carries the oil into the joints.
  • A dry, red, or black chain is a chain that is elongating right now. Lube it, and then find out why the lube schedule failed.
  • Enclosed drives with an oil bath need the oil level checked and changed like a gearbox.
Tensioning: too little slips, too much kills bearings
The right tension is the lowest tension that does not slip under full load
Every extra newton of belt tension is a newton of radial load on the motor and driven bearings, all the time, whether the drive is working or not. Over-tensioning is the most common way a belt drive kills the bearings on both machines. Under-tensioning slips, glazes, and burns belts. Over-tensioning fails bearings and stretches belts. The window between is narrower than it feels by hand.
V-belt tension by deflection
The standard field method. Measure the span (the free length of belt between the sheaves), apply a force at the middle of the span perpendicular to the belt, and measure how far it deflects. The target deflection is proportional to the span; the target force comes from the belt manufacturer's table for the belt section and small sheave diameter.
Target deflection = span length ÷ 64
(1/64 in per inch of span, or 1.6 mm per 100 mm of span)

Example: 800 mm span → deflect 12.5 mm at mid-span
Apply the manufacturer's deflection force for the section and sheave size
with a spring scale or belt tension gauge. Read the force at 12.5 mm.
Below the table minimum: tighten. Above the maximum: loosen.
  • The force values are in the belt catalogue and on most manufacturer websites. They vary with section, small sheave diameter, and speed. Do not guess them; a spring scale and the table takes two minutes.
  • New belts are tensioned to the higher "new belt" value and rechecked after a run-in period (typically 24 to 48 hours of operation) when the initial stretch has occurred and the belts have seated in the grooves.
  • Sonic tension meters measure the belt's natural frequency when plucked and convert to tension. Fast, repeatable, and the only practical method on serpentine and synchronous belts. Worth having if you maintain many drives.
  • Banded belts and multiple belt sets are tensioned as a set: the deflection force is multiplied by the number of belts.
  • Rotate the drive a few turns by hand after adjusting and check tension again. The belt seats and the reading changes.
Synchronous belt tension
Timing belts do not slip, so the tension is not about grip. It is about keeping the belt teeth seated so they do not ratchet (jump teeth) under load, without pulling the belt cords or the bearings apart. Too loose ratchets and shreds teeth. Too tight whines and fails the cords. The window is set by the manufacturer for the belt pitch and width, and a sonic meter or the manufacturer's deflection force method is used.
  • Never tension a timing belt by feel. The correct tension often feels loose compared to a V-belt.
  • Timing belts run on flanged pulleys. A belt walking off a flange is a misalignment or a damaged flange, not a tension problem.
  • Never force a timing belt over a pulley flange. The tension cords are glass or aramid and a kink or a forced fit breaks them invisibly. Move the tensioner or the motor.
Chain tension by sag
A chain drive runs with a slack strand and a tight strand. The slack strand should sag, and the sag is how tension is set. Too tight loads the bearings and accelerates pin wear. Too loose lets the chain whip, climb the sprocket teeth, and hammer.
Horizontal or slightly inclined drives: sag = 2 to 4% of span
Vertical or steep drives: sag = 1 to 2% of span (the chain cannot hang off the lower sprocket)

Example: 1000 mm centre distance, horizontal → 20 to 40 mm sag on the slack strand
Measure by pulling the slack strand taut with a straightedge, then measure the drop at mid-span.
  • The slack strand should be on the bottom on horizontal drives. If the drive reverses, both strands alternate and the sag target is at the low end of the range.
  • Idler sprockets or tensioners take up slack on fixed-centre drives. An idler runs on the slack strand, on the outside of the chain, and needs its own lubrication and its own bearing check.
  • A chain that needs retensioning at every PM is elongating. Measure it (Failure Analysis tab). Taking up slack does not fix a worn chain; it just delays the day it jumps the sprocket.
  • Chain tension changes with temperature on long drives. Set it at operating temperature.
Field tip: tension the drive, then feel the motor bearingsAfter tensioning any belt drive, run it and put an infrared thermometer or your hand near the motor drive-end bearing after twenty minutes. A bearing that is warmer than the opposite end is telling you the belt tension is higher than it needs to be. The belt table says one thing; the bearing says what the machine actually sees.
Sheave and sprocket alignment
Belt and chain drives are more tolerant of misalignment than couplings, which is one reason they get used. That tolerance is why they are also more often installed misaligned and left that way. A misaligned belt drive wears one side of the belt, throws rubber, runs hot, and can roll the belt over or off. A misaligned chain drive wears the inside of the side plates and one face of the sprocket teeth, and eventually the chain climbs off.
Three kinds of sheave misalignment
TypeWhat it isSymptomCorrection
Offset (parallel)Shafts parallel, sheaves not in the same planeBelt wear on one sidewall, belt runs to one side of the grooveMove one sheave along its shaft
Angular (horizontal)Shafts not parallel when viewed from aboveBelt enters the groove at an angle, wears one side, squeals at one sheaveMove one end of one machine sideways
Angular (vertical, twist)Shafts not parallel when viewed from the side; sheave faces not in the same planeBelt twists between sheaves, turns over, wears both sides unevenlyShim one machine to bring shafts parallel
  • Straightedge method: lay a straightedge across both sheave faces. It should touch each sheave at two points, four total. Any gap shows offset or angular error. Works when both sheaves have the same face width; correct for the difference if they do not.
  • String method for long centre distances: the same idea with a taut string.
  • Laser sheave alignment tools clip into a groove and project a line onto the other sheave. Fast and accurate and worth it on drives that eat belts.
  • General tolerance: within half a degree of angular error, or about 1 mm of offset per 100 mm of centre distance, is the maximum most belt manufacturers accept. Tighter is better. Synchronous belts want a quarter of that.
  • Check alignment after tensioning, not before. Tensioning pulls the motor on its slide base and can change the alignment.
Sprocket alignment
Sprockets must be in the same plane and the shafts must be parallel. Chain does not tolerate twist at all: a chain running with the sprockets out of plane wears the inside of the side plates on one side and puts a bending load on the pins. The straightedge method across both sprocket faces is the standard check.
  • Sprocket run-out: with the shaft locked, rotate the sprocket against a dial indicator on the tooth tips (radial) and on the face (axial). Excessive run-out on a bored-to-size sprocket usually means the bore is off centre or the key is fighting the fit.
  • Taper-lock and QD bushings must be seated and torqued in sequence. A bushing that walks changes the alignment and eventually spins on the shaft.
  • Shaft parallelism is checked with a feeler gauge or dial indicator between the shafts at both ends, or with the straightedge across the sprocket faces at top and bottom.
Centre distance and belt length
Belt pitch length ≈ 2C + 1.57 × (D + d) + (D - d)² ÷ (4C)
C = centre distance, D and d = large and small pitch diameters

Chain length in pitches ≈ 2C/P + (N + n)/2 + P × (N - n)² ÷ (39.5 × C)
C = centre distance, P = pitch, N and n = sprocket tooth counts. Round up to an even number.
Centre distance has to allow take-up: room to move the motor closer to fit the belt without forcing it, and room to move it away to tension and to take up stretch over the belt's life. A drive with no take-up left is a drive that cannot be tensioned correctly and is about to be replaced.
Field tip: never pry a belt onRolling a V-belt onto a sheave with a screwdriver or pry bar looks quick and breaks the tension cords inside the belt where you cannot see it. The belt will fail early and you will blame the belt. Loosen the motor, slip the belt on by hand, then tension it. If there is not enough take-up to do that, the drive is designed wrong or the wrong belt length was ordered.

Chain elongation: the measurement that decides replacement
Chain does not stretch. The pins and bushings wear, each joint gets a little longer, and the sum over the length of the chain is elongation. As the chain elongates its pitch no longer matches the sprocket, it rides up the teeth toward the tips, hammers, and eventually jumps. Replacement is at 3 percent elongation for most drives, and at 1.5 percent for large sprockets (roughly 68 teeth and above) and precision timing applications, because a large sprocket tolerates less pitch error.
Pull the chain taut (slack strand, or off the drive) and measure over 12 pitches, pin centre to pin centre
Nominal length = 12 × pitch
Elongation % = (measured - nominal) ÷ nominal × 100

Example: #60 chain, pitch 19.05 mm, 12 pitches = 228.6 mm nominal
Measured 235.0 mm → (235.0 - 228.6) ÷ 228.6 = 2.8% → replace soon
Measured 236.5 mm → 3.5% → replace now
  • Measure in three places around the chain. Wear is rarely even.
  • Chain wear gauges (a go/no-go bar marked for each pitch) make this a ten second check.
  • A new chain on worn sprockets wears fast to match the sprockets. If the sprocket teeth are hooked, replace the sprockets with the chain.
  • Stiff links that do not articulate freely are a sign of corrosion, dirt in the joint, or a bent pin. A stiff chain fails on the stiff link.
Sprocket wear: read the teeth
  • Hooked or shark-fin teeth: the tooth face is worn into a hook shape on the loaded side. This is normal wear at the end of a sprocket's life and it holds the chain on so well that the chain will not come off even when it should. A hooked sprocket wears a new chain fast.
  • Wear on one face of the teeth only: misalignment.
  • Polished tooth tips with the roots unworn: the chain is elongated and riding high.
  • Chipped or broken teeth: shock load, or the chain jumped and landed hard. Look for the cause upstream.
  • Small sprockets wear faster than large ones because each tooth engages more often. A 17 to 25 tooth driver is the practical minimum for smooth running; below about 17 teeth the chain speed varies enough on each tooth (chordal action) to be a vibration and noise source.
Field tip: keep the old beltBefore the failed belt goes in the bin, look at it under a light, then put it beside the new one. Length, section, and any markings. Half of short-belt-life complaints are a belt that was never the right belt. The other half are on this tab.
Belt and chain drive fault diagnosis

Lock out first. Belt and chain drives are the nip point hazard in the plant. Nothing on this tab is done with the guard off and the drive live.

Under the guard, before touching: rubber dust (slip or misalignment), metal dust or paste on a chain (dry chain), belt ride height in the groove (worn sheave), tensioner position (how much take-up is left), and anything foreign in the guard.

Tension: deflection and force on belts, sag on chain. Compare to the table. Note whether it is high, low, or right, because the next steps depend on it.

Alignment: straightedge across the sheave or sprocket faces. Four points of contact or it is out.

Condition: belt sidewalls, underside, cover. Chain elongation over 12 pitches. Sprocket tooth profile. Sheave groove gauge.

Bearings: both shafts, both ends, by hand with the drive locked out: play, roughness. Then by infrared after a run: the drive-end bearing running warmer than the opposite end is over-tension.

Then run it and listen. Squeal at startup versus under load versus continuous tells you different things. Screwdriver stethoscope on the bearing housings if the plant is loud.

Power transmission safety: nip points and stored energy
Belts and chains are the nip point hazard in the plant
The point where a belt or chain enters a sheave or sprocket is an in-running nip. It pulls in anything it catches: a finger, a sleeve, a glove, hair, a rag, a lanyard. At belt speeds of hundreds of metres per minute there is no reaction time. Guards stay on when the drive is running. Every time. No exceptions for a quick look. Ontario Regulation 851 sections 24 and 25 require in-running nip points and exposed moving parts to be guarded. A drive found running with the guard off is a stop-work condition.
Lockout before the guard comes off
  • Lock out the drive motor. Verify by attempting a start. Then remove the guard.
  • Driven equipment can turn the drive backward: a fan windmilling, a conveyor with material on an incline, a gravity load. Block or pin the driven side before putting hands on the belt or chain.
  • A belt drive under tension is a spring. A chain drive with a spring tensioner is a spring. Relieve tension before disconnecting a belt or breaking a chain, and know where your hands are when it lets go.
  • Never rotate a drive by hand with fingers on the belt or chain. Turn it by the shaft, the coupling, or a bar in a sheave spoke, with hands clear of the nip.
  • Guards go back on before the lock comes off. Check that nothing was left inside: tools, rags, the old belt.
Belt and chain handling
  • Do not pry belts on or off. It damages the belt and it puts your hand next to the nip when the belt snaps into the groove.
  • Chain breakers and chain pullers are the tools for breaking and joining chain. A hammer and punch on a chain pin under tension is how fingers get broken and pins get launched.
  • A spring clip on a master link opens toward the direction of travel. Installed backward, it can be stripped off by contact and the chain comes apart at speed.
  • Taper-lock bushings can bind and release suddenly. Loosen the screws fully, use the jacking holes, and keep fingers clear of the hub gap.
  • Belt dust and chain lube on the floor are slip hazards. Clean up under the guard as part of the job.
Heat, fire, and chemicals
  • A slipping belt generates enough heat to smoke and, in a dusty guard, to ignite. Belt smell from a guard is an immediate stop.
  • Chain lubricant sprays are flammable aerosols. Not near hot surfaces, not near welding.
  • Belt dressing is a slip cure that shortens belt life and can make a belt grab and throw. Fix the tension or the sheave instead.
  • Static: fast-running belts can build a static charge. In dusty or solvent atmospheres, static conductive belts and a grounded frame are required.
Guard design basics for the person who has to make one
Millwrights build and modify guards. A guard that is hard to remove for maintenance gets left off. A guard that is easy to defeat gets defeated. CSA Z432 is the reference for machine guarding in Canada.
  • Fully enclose the drive, including the return side of the belt or chain and both sheaves or sprockets.
  • Mesh openings sized to the reach distance to the nearest hazard (Z432 tables). Small openings close to the nip; larger openings are allowed further away.
  • Fixed guards need a tool to remove. Hinged guards need an interlock if they can be opened without a tool.
  • Provide an inspection window or a hinged access panel for tension checks so the whole guard does not need to come off for a look.
  • The guard must not be a hazard itself: no sharp edges, no pinch points at the hinge, and rigid enough that it cannot be pushed into the drive.
